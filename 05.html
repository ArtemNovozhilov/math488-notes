5.1Preliminaries

Now it is time to put aside our computers and try to look at the methods to solve numerically scalar equations from a somewhat deeper theoretical point of view.

Recall that our goal is to analyze the equation of the form \begin{equation}\label{eq5:1} f(x)=0,\quad x\in \R, \end{equation} where \(f\) is a given function. For the following I will assume that this function is at least continuous (and, if necessary, differentiable as many times as needed for my reasoning). I write “analyze” and not “solve,” because I remind you again that the very first question for any mathematical problem is not “How to solve it?” but “Does a solution exist?” We already had one sufficient condition for \(f\) to have a root on \([a,b]\) (Theorem 3.1), namely, if \(f\) is continuous and \(f(a)f(b)<0\) then there must be a point \(c\in(a,b)\) such that \(f(c)=0\). Here is another useful similar statement (which can be even called a “baby version of Brouwer’s fixed point theorem”).

Theorem 5.1. Let \(g\colon[a,b]\longrightarrow [a,b]\) be a continuous function. Then there exists \(\hat x\in[a,b]\) such that \begin{equation}\label{eq5:2} \hat x=g(\hat x). \end{equation}

Quite naturally, such \(\hat x\) is called a fixed point of \(g\). The key thing here is to note that \(g\) maps closed interval into itself.

Proof. Consider \[ f(x)=x-g(x), \] which is a continuous function as the difference of two continuous functions.

I have \[ f(a)=a-g(a)\leq 0,\quad f(b)=b-g(b)\geq 0, \] and hence \(f(a)f(b)\leq 0\). Therefore \(f\) is zero either at \(a\) or at \(b\), or, by Bolzano’s theorem (Theorem 3.1), at some point inside \([a,b]\). In other words, there is \(\hat x\in[a,b]\) such that \(f(\hat x)=0\) or \[ \hat x=g(\hat x) \] as required. ■

Why do we care about equations of the form \(x=g(x)\)? The short answer goes as follows: I start with \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) and transform it into \(\eqref{eq5:2}\). Now my goal will be to find a fixed point of \(g\). My hope is that if I start with some good \(x_0\), I can find \(x_1=g(x_0)\), then \(x_2=g(x_1)\), i.e., use \(\eqref{eq5:2}\) as a recurrent formula (we already saw such formulas in Section 4), and produce the sequence of simple iterates \(x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots\) If this sequence converges to a fixed point of \(g\), then this fixed point will be a root of \(f\) and I am done.

First I remark that there are always different ways to go from \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) to \(\eqref{eq5:2}\) including the obvious one \[ f(x)=0\implies x+f(x)=x\implies g(x)=x,\,g(x)=x+f(x). \] Some of them work and some do not as the following example shows.

Example 5.2. Let \[ f(x)=e^x-2x-1. \] If you make a graph of this function, you can see that there must be a root somewhere in \([1,2]\). Indeed \[ f(1)=e-3<0,\quad f(2)=e^2-5>0, \] and \(f\) is continuous. Let me try first to transform \(f(x)=0\) into \(g(x)=x\) naively: \[ x=\frac{e^x-1}{2}=g(x). \] I need \(g\) to map \([1,2]\) into \([1,2]\), but the first check \(g(1)=(e-1)/2<1\) fails. So this will not work.

Now I try the following: \[ e^x=2x+1\implies x=\log (2x+1)=g(x) \] (recall that \(\log\) denotes the natural logarithm). Now \(g(1)=\log 3>1,\,g(2)=\log 5<2\), and \(\log\) is a monotone function, there are no hills and valleys. Hence \[ g\colon[1,2]\longrightarrow [1,2], \] and I know (thanks to Theorem 5.1) that there must be a fixed point of \(g\) in \([1,2]\). So I can try to build my sequence of iterates \(x_0,x_1,\ldots\), but first I would like to find a sufficient condition that this sequence converges prior to performing actual calculations.

5.2Contraction mapping principle

Definition 5.3. Function \(g\colon[a,b]\longrightarrow [a,b]\) is called a contraction on \([a,b]\) if there exists a constant \(r\), \(0\leq r<1\) such that \begin{equation}\label{eq5:3} |g(x)-g(y)|\leq r|x-y| \end{equation} for any \(x,y\in[a,b]\).

Since \(|x-y|\) measures the distance between \(x\) and \(y\) then contraction means geometrically that the distance between the images of \(x,y\) under function \(g\) is strictly less than the distance between \(x\) and \(y\). If I iterate \(g\), then at each step I contract the distance from my current iterate to the unknown fixed point, so intuitively it is expected that in the end I will arrive at this point! Now let me convince you that this intuition is indeed correct.

Theorem 5.4 (Contraction mapping principle). Let \(g\colon[a,b]\longrightarrow[a,b]\) and \(g\) be a contraction. Then \(g\) has a unique fixed point \(\hat x\in[a,b]\), and, moreover, for any \(x_0\in[a,b]\) the sequence of simple iterates \(x_0,x_1,\ldots\) obtained through the recurrence \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n),\,n=0,1,2,\ldots\) converges to \(\hat x\). Additionally, the following estimate holds \begin{equation}\label{eq5:4} |x_n-\hat x|\leq \frac{r^n}{1-r}|x_1-x_0|\,. \end{equation}

In the proof I will use a few basic mathematical facts, which you saw in other classes. Let me recall them here. First, sequence \((x_n)\) is said to converge to \(\hat x\) (is said to have the limit \(\hat x\)) if for any \(\varepsilon >0\) there is natural number \(N\) such that for all \(n\geq N\) I have \(|x_n-\hat x|<\varepsilon\). For instance sequence \(x_n=1\) converges to 1, sequence \(x_n=\frac{1}{n}\) converges to 0, and sequence \(x_n=(-1)^n\) has no limit (does not converge, this sequence diverges). In particular, I will need the fact that the sequence \(x_n=Ar^n\) for any constant \(A\) and \(0\leq r<1\) converges to 0.

Second, the absolute value satisfies the triangle inequality \[ |x+y|\leq |x|+|y|. \] Now I am ready for a proof.

Proof. First, I will prove uniqueness. Assume that I have two fixed points \(\hat x=g(\hat x), \,\hat y=g(\hat y)\) in \([a,b]\). Then \[ |\hat x-\hat y|=|g(\hat x)-g(\hat y)|\leq r|\hat x-\hat y| \] since \(g\) is a contraction. Or, \[ (1-r)|\hat x-\hat y|\leq 0. \] Since \(1-r>0\) by assumption I must have \(|\hat x-\hat y|=0\) (absolute value cannot be negative) and hence \(\hat x=\hat y\).

Now to the existence. Note that a contraction is automatically continuous: if \(|x-y|<\delta\) then \(|g(x)-g(y)|\leq r|x-y|<\delta\), so in the definition of continuity one can take \(\delta=\epsilon\). Hence by Theorem 5.1 I know that a fixed point \(\hat x\) must exist (this is a pure existence statement without indication how to find this fixed point). Now I will present a constructive approach to the fixed point. I consider a sequence of iterates \[ x_{n+1}=g(x_n),\quad n=0,1,2,3,\ldots, \] starting with some arbitrary \(x_0\in[a,b]\). Let me estimate \begin{align*} |x_n-\hat x|&=|g(x_{n-1})-g(\hat x)|\leq r|x_{n-1}-\hat x|= \\ &= r|g(x_{n-2})-g(\hat x)|\leq r^2|x_{n-2}-\hat x|\leq \ldots\\ &\leq r^{n-1}|x_1-\hat x|\leq r^n|x_0-\hat x|. \end{align*} Since \(r^n\to 0\) I conclude that \(x_n\to\hat x\) as required.

Moreover, \[ |x_1-\hat x|\leq r|x_0-\hat x|= r|x_0-x_1+x_1-\hat x|\leq r|x_0-x_1|+r|x_1-\hat x| \] implies that \[ |x_1-\hat x|\leq\frac{r}{1-r}|x_1-x_0|, \] which, together with \(|x_n-\hat x|\leq r^{n-1}|x_1-\hat x|\), finishes the proof: \[ |x_n-\hat x|\leq \frac{r^n}{1-r}|x_1-x_0|. \] ■

Remark 5.5. The contraction mapping principle is important for two main reasons (in addition to guaranteeing the existence and uniqueness of a fixed point). The first reason is that it provides a constructive way to approach the fixed point: start with some \(x_0\) and produce a sequence. It must converge to \(\hat x\). The second reason is that it allows estimating the error we make. For instance, assume that we want that \[ |x_n-\hat x|\leq\varepsilon. \] (Say, if I need \(k\) correct digits, I would request that \(\varepsilon = 0.5\cdot 10^{-k}\)). Then I can calculate \[ \frac{r^n}{1-r}|x_1-x_0|\leq \varepsilon\implies n\geq\frac{\log\frac{\varepsilon(1-r)}{|x_1-x_0|}}{\log r}\,. \]

Finally, I need a relatively simple procedure to see if my function is actually a contraction. For this I will need the mean value theorem (MVT) from Calculus I. I will state it without proof, you can consult any calculus book for this.

Theorem 5.6 (MVT). Let \(g\colon [a,b]\longrightarrow \R\) be a continuously differentiable function. Then for any \(a\leq x<y\leq b\) there is point \(c\in[x,y]\) such that \[ \frac{g(y)-g(x)}{y-x}=g'(c). \]

This theorem has a simple geometric meaning: No matter which points \(x\) and \(y\) you will take and draw a secant line connecting the points \((x,g(x))\) and \((y,g(y))\), you can always find a point between \(x\) and \(y\) such that the tangent line to the graph of \(g\) at this point has exactly the same slope as this secant line.

Now to the contraction. From the mean value theorem it follows that \[ |g(y)-g(x)|=|g'(c)||y-x|. \] Now let \(r=\max_{c\in[a,b]}|g'(c)|\), which must exist since \(g'\) is a continuous function on a closed interval. If I can show that \(0\leq r<1\) I conclude that \[ |g(y)-g(x)|\leq r|y-x|, \] for any \(x,y\in[a,b]\), i.e., \(g\) is a contraction on \([a,b]\).

Example 5.7. Let me continue Example 5.2. I have that \[ x=g(x)=\log(2x+1), \] and \(g\colon[1,2]\longrightarrow[1,2]\). Now I want to check that \(g\) is a contraction.

Indeed, I have \[ g'(x)=\frac{2}{2x+1}\,,\quad g''(x)=-\frac{4}{(2x+1)^2}\,, \] which implies that \(g'\) is positive (so I do not have to care about the absolute value) and monotonically decreasing on \([1,2]\) (\(g''<0\)), and hence it attains its maximum at the point \(x=1\): \[ \max_{x\in[1,2]}|g'(x)|=\frac 23<1, \] and hence for any \(x,y\in[1,2]\) \[ |g(x)-g(y)|\leq\frac 23|x-y|, \] i.e., \(g\) is a contraction. This implies, according to the theorem I proved, that if I take any \(x_0\) from \([1,2]\) my sequence \((x_n)\) will converge to the unique fixed point. Moreover, if I want to make sure that my absolute error does not exceed \(0.5\cdot 10^{-6}\) (I hope to have approximately 6 correct digits after the decimal point), then, using my formula above, \[ n\geq \frac{\log \frac{\varepsilon(1-r)}{|x_1-x_0|}}{\log r}\,, \] which gives \(n\geq 32.78\), i.e., \(n\geq 33\), for \(r=2/3\), \(x_0=1\), \(x_1=g(1)=\log 3\approx 1.0986\). This is, of course, a pessimistic estimate: since I use inequalities in all my reasoning, it is only an upper bound for the number of iterations, which does not have to be attained. Let me check it numerically. To know the error, I need \(\hat x\); I find it by performing many more iterations than necessary (the theorem guarantees that this works). Then I iterate again, starting from \(x_0=1\), until the error does not exceed \(\varepsilon\), and print every fourth iterate.

# Simple iterations x_n = g(x_{n-1}) for g(x) = log(2x + 1), starting from x_0 = 1

import math


def g(x):
    return math.log(2 * x + 1)


x_hat = 1.0
for n in range(100):    # many more iterations than necessary
    x_hat = g(x_hat)

eps = 0.5e-6            # the required accuracy
x = 1.0                 # x_0
n = 0
while abs(x - x_hat) > eps:
    x = g(x)
    n += 1
    if n % 4 == 0:
        print(f"n = {n:2d}   x_n = {x:.10f}   error = {abs(x - x_hat):.2e}")

print(f"The error does not exceed {eps} for n = {n}")
n =  4   x_n = 1.2245628908   error = 3.19e-02
n =  8   x_n = 1.2530183536   error = 3.41e-03
n = 12   x_n = 1.2560718971   error = 3.59e-04
n = 16   x_n = 1.2563934480   error = 3.78e-05
n = 20   x_n = 1.2564272411   error = 3.97e-06
n = 24   x_n = 1.2564307918   error = 4.17e-07
The error does not exceed 5e-07 for n = 24

So \(n=24\) iterations are sufficient to find the fixed point \(\hat x\approx 1.256431\), and hence the root of \(f(x)=0\), with the required precision. Note also that every four iterations the error decreases approximately ten times; I will explain this in Section 5.4.

Finally, I want a somewhat simpler criterion to guarantee that my iterates converge. As usual, I have to pay a price for this. Now it will be a local statement (which means it is true only for some neighborhood of \(\hat x\)), which will follow directly from the general contraction mapping principle.

Corollary 5.8. Let \(g\) be continuously differentiable on \([a,b]\) and have a fixed point \(\hat x\in(a,b)\). If \(|g'(\hat x)|<1\), then there is a neighborhood \(I=[\hat x-\delta,\hat x+\delta]\subset[a,b]\) with some \(\delta>0\) such that the sequence \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\) converges to \(\hat x\) for any \(x_0\in I\).

Proof. Since \(|g'(\hat x)|<1\) and \(g'\) is continuous, there must be an interval \(I=[\hat x-\delta,\hat x+\delta]\subset[a,b]\), \(\delta>0\), where \(|g'(x)|\leq r<1\) for some constant \(r\). By the mean value theorem, \(|g(x)-g(y)|\leq r|x-y|\) for any \(x,y\in I\). Moreover, \(g\) maps \(I\) into itself: for any \(x\in I\) \[ |g(x)-\hat x|=|g(x)-g(\hat x)|\leq r|x-\hat x|\leq r\delta<\delta. \] Hence \(g\colon I\longrightarrow I\) is a contraction on \(I\), and by Theorem 5.4 any sequence \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\) starting in \(I\) converges to the unique fixed point of \(g\) in \(I\), which is \(\hat x\). ■

Remark 5.9. Very naturally, any fixed point of \(g\) for which \(|g'(\hat x)|<1\) holds is called stable fixed point or sink. Analogously, I can define an unstable fixed point \(\hat x\) or a source of \(g\) such that \(|g'(\hat x)|>1\): for \(x_0\) close to such \(\hat x\) the iterates move away from it. If \(|g'(\hat x)|=1\), no conclusion can be made without further analysis. The behavior of the iterates near sinks and sources is best seen with the graphical analysis in Section 5.3.

For example, the function \(g(x)=(e^x-1)/2\) from Example 5.2, which I rejected, has two fixed points: \(\hat x=0\), which is also a root of \(f(x)=e^x-2x-1\), and the root \(\hat x\approx 1.2564\) that I am looking for. Since \(g'(x)=e^x/2\), the first one is a sink (\(g'(0)=1/2\)), and the second one is a source (\(g'(1.2564)\approx 1.76\)). Hence the simple iterates with this \(g\) either converge to the “wrong” root \(0\), or go away to infinity, but never converge to the root in \([1,2]\) (unless \(x_0\) is exactly this root).

5.3Graphical analysis

There is a simple way to see the simple iterates \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\). I draw the graph of \(y=g(x)\) and the straight line \(y=x\); their intersections are exactly the fixed points of \(g\). Starting with \(x_0\), I go vertically to the graph of \(g\) and arrive at the point \((x_0,g(x_0))=(x_0,x_1)\). Then I go horizontally to the line \(y=x\) and arrive at the point \((x_1,x_1)\), that is, right above (or below) \(x_1\). Now I repeat: vertically to the graph, which gives \((x_1,x_2)\), horizontally to the line, which gives \((x_2,x_2)\), etc. The resulting picture is called a cobweb diagram.

In Fig. 5.1(a) you can see the cobweb diagram for \(g(x)=\log(2x+1)\) from Example 5.7 with \(x_0=1\) and with \(x_0=2\). Since \(0<g'(x)<1\), the iterates approach the fixed point monotonically, from one side, and the diagram looks like a staircase. If \(-1<g'(\hat x)<0\), the iterates approach \(\hat x\) jumping from one side of it to the other, and the diagram looks like a spiral; Fig. 5.1(b) shows this for \(g(x)=\cos x\), whose fixed point \(\hat x\approx 0.739\) is a sink with \(g'(\hat x)=-\sin\hat x\approx -0.67\).

Finally, Fig. 5.2 shows the cobweb diagram for \(g(x)=(e^x-1)/2\) from Remark 5.9. The iterates starting at \(x_0=1.2\), slightly to the left of the source \(\hat x\approx 1.2564\), move away from it and converge to the sink \(0\), whereas the iterates starting at \(x_0=1.3\), slightly to the right of the source, move away from it to infinity. In general, near a fixed point the graph of \(g\) is close to the tangent line with the slope \(g'(\hat x)\), and the cobweb diagram shows that the iterates approach \(\hat x\) if this line is less steep than the line \(y=x\) (i.e., \(|g'(\hat x)|<1\)), and move away from \(\hat x\) if it is steeper (i.e., \(|g'(\hat x)|>1\)).

5.4Rate of convergence

How fast do my simple iterates approach the fixed point? Recall that the formal mathematical notion for this is the order and rate of convergence (Definition 4.1).

Let \[ \varepsilon_n=|x_n-\hat x| \] be the absolute error at the \(n\)-th step, and assume that \(x_n\neq\hat x\) for all \(n\) (otherwise there is nothing to discuss). I have \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\varepsilon_n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|x_{n+1}-\hat x|}{|x_n-\hat x|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|g(x_{n})-g(\hat x)|}{|x_n-\hat x|}=|g'(\hat x)|, \] where I again used the mean value theorem: the ratio under the last limit is \(|g'(c_n)|\) for some \(c_n\) between \(x_n\) and \(\hat x\), and \(c_n\to\hat x\), since \(x_n\to\hat x\). Hence I conclude that, if \(0<|g'(\hat x)|<1\), simple iterates have the linear order of convergence with the rate of convergence \[ \mu=|g'(\hat x)|. \] If \(g'(\hat x)=0\), the convergence is faster than linear (superlinear, see the remark below). This is exactly what happens for the Newton–Raphson method, which is the simple iteration with \(g(x)=x-f(x)/f'(x)\) (check that \(g'(\hat x)=0\) if \(f'(\hat x)\neq 0\)). This value has a direct interpretation. Namely, \[ \rho = -\log_{10}\mu \] gives (asymptotically!) the number of correct digits gained in one iteration. Or, equivalently, the quantity \[ \left\lceil\frac{1}{\rho}\right\rceil, \] where \(\lceil\cdot\rceil\) is the ceiling function (the smallest integer that is not smaller than its argument), is the number of iterations to gain at least one more correct digit.

Recall, that the bisection method has the rate of convergence \(\mu=1/2\), hence \[ \rho=-\log_{10}\frac 12\approx 0.301,\quad \lceil1/\rho\rceil=\lceil 3.32\ldots\rceil=4, \] i.e., for the bisection method one needs to perform 4 steps on average to gain another correct digit. For the simple iterations in Example 5.7 I have \(\mu=g'(\hat x)=2/(2\hat x+1)\approx 0.569\), hence \(\rho\approx 0.245\) and \(\lceil1/\rho\rceil=\lceil 4.09\ldots\rceil=5\). This agrees with the output of my code: every four iterations the error decreases by the factor \(\mu^4\approx 0.105\), that is, slightly less than ten times.

Remark 5.10. In the definition of the rate of convergence it is possible that \(\mu=1\) or \(\mu=0\) and the order is linear. In the former case we say that the convergence is sublinear, in the latter case — superlinear. Ideally I want a method that would have \(\mu\) as close as possible to zero. For any other than linear order the rate of convergence \(\mu\) does not give much information (since it changes if we rescale the variable \(x\), think this out).
