6.1Convergence order of the Newton–Raphson method

Now I am well prepared to give a precise mathematical explanation why the Newton–Raphson method approaches the sought root so much faster (compare with, say, the bisection method). But first I would like to start with a very general definition.

Definition 6.1. Let \(f\) be continuous. Relaxation uses the sequence \[ x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\lambda f(x_n),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots, \] where \(\lambda\neq 0\) is a constant to be determined.

In the relaxation sequence above I have simple iterations with \[ g(x)=x-\lambda f(x), \] whose fixed points are exactly the roots of \(f\). According to the discussion in the previous section, the rate of convergence of simple iterations is \(\mu=|g'(\hat x)|\), and I would like to make it as close as possible to 0. Here I have \[ g'(\hat x)=1-\lambda f'(\hat x), \] so the optimal choice is \[ \lambda=\frac{1}{f'(\hat x)}\,, \] which gives \(g'(\hat x)=0\). The trouble is that I do not know \(\hat x\). Hence I replace \(\hat x\) with the best approximation of it that I have at the current step, namely, with \(x_n\), which means that \(\lambda\) is no longer a constant and changes from step to step. In this way I end up with the familiar recurrent formula for the Newton–Raphson method \begin{equation}\label{eq6:1} x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\,. \end{equation} This is still a simple iteration \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\), now with \(g(x)=x-f(x)/f'(x)\), and, as you were asked to check in Section 5, \[ g'(x)=\frac{f(x)f''(x)}{\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)^2}\,,\quad\text{hence}\quad g'(\hat x)=0\quad\text{if}\quad f'(\hat x)\neq 0. \] Hence the convergence of the Newton–Raphson method is superlinear. Let me find its order.

For the following I will need to recall Taylor’s formula.

Let \(f\colon (a,b)\longrightarrow \R\) be a \((p+1)\) times continuously differentiable function and let \(c\in(a,b)\). Then it can be proved that for any \(x\in(a,b)\) \[ f(x)=f(c)+f'(c)(x-c)+\frac{f''(c)}{2!}(x-c)^2+\ldots+\frac{f^{(p)}(c)}{p!}(x-c)^p+\frac{f^{(p+1)}(\xi)}{(p+1)!}(x-c)^{p+1}, \] where \(\xi\) is some point between \(x\) and \(c\). The constant \(c\) is called the center of Taylor’s formula, and the last term is called the remainder. The main profit here is that we replace our function \(f\) with “almost” a polynomial, which becomes exactly a polynomial if I drop the remainder.

Now let \(\hat x\) be a root of \(f\), i.e., \(f(\hat x)=0\). Assuming that \(f\) is a twice continuously differentiable function, I can write Taylor’s formula with the center at the \(n\)-th iterate \(x_n\): \[ 0=f(\hat x)=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(\hat x-x_n)+\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2!}(\hat x-x_n)^2, \] where \(\xi_n\) is between \(\hat x\) and \(x_n\).

From \(\eqref{eq6:1}\) I have that \[ f(x_n)=f'(x_n)(x_{n}-x_{n+1}), \] which, together with Taylor’s formula, gives \(0=f'(x_n)(\hat x-x_{n+1})+\frac{1}{2}f''(\xi_n)(\hat x-x_n)^2\), or \begin{equation}\label{eq6:2} x_{n+1}-\hat x=\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2f'(x_n)}(x_n-\hat x)^2. \end{equation} Passing to the absolute values and using the usual notation for the absolute errors \(\varepsilon_n=|x_n-\hat x|\), I can rewrite \(\eqref{eq6:2}\) as \begin{equation}\label{eq6:3} \varepsilon_{n+1}=\left|\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2f'(x_n)}\right|\varepsilon_n^2. \end{equation} Now I will assume that \(f'(\hat x)\neq 0\). Since \(f'\) is continuous, \(f'(x)\neq 0\) in some neighborhood of \(\hat x\), and since \(f''\) is also continuous, there is a closed interval \(I=[\hat x-\delta,\hat x+\delta]\) for some \(\delta>0\) and a constant \(M\) such that \[ \left|\frac{f''(y)}{2f'(x)}\right|\leq M \] for all \(x,y\in I\). Moreover, decreasing \(\delta\) if necessary, I can assume that \(M\delta<1\) (when \(\delta\) decreases, the smallest possible \(M\) does not increase).

Let \(x_0\in I\), i.e., \(\varepsilon_0\leq\delta\). Then \(\xi_0\in I\) and \(\eqref{eq6:3}\) gives \[ \varepsilon_{1}\leq M\varepsilon_0^2=(M\varepsilon_0)\varepsilon_0<\varepsilon_0, \] hence \(x_1\in I\) as well. Repeating this argument (that is, by induction), all the iterates stay in \(I\), and I get the estimate \[ \varepsilon_{n+1}\leq M\varepsilon_n^2,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots \] In words, the Newton–Raphson method (approximately) squares the error at each step. Moreover, from the last formula \begin{align*} \varepsilon_{n+1}&\leq M\varepsilon_n^2\leq M(M\varepsilon_{n-1}^2)^2=\frac{1}{M}(M\varepsilon_{n-1})^{4}\leq\frac{1}{M}(M(M\varepsilon_{n-2}^2))^{4}=\frac{1}{M}(M\varepsilon_{n-2})^{8}\leq\ldots\\ &\leq \frac{1}{M}(M\varepsilon_0)^{2^{n+1}}, \end{align*} and therefore \(\varepsilon_n\to 0\), since \(M\varepsilon_0<1\). Finally, since \(x_n\to\hat x\) and \(\xi_n\) lies between \(x_n\) and \(\hat x\), also \(\xi_n\to\hat x\), and \(\eqref{eq6:3}\) gives \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\varepsilon_n^2}=\left|\frac{f''(\hat x)}{2f'(\hat x)}\right| \] (assuming that \(x_n\neq\hat x\) for all \(n\)). If \(f''(\hat x)\neq 0\), according to Definition 4.1, the order of convergence is two, and the rate of convergence is \(\mu=|f''(\hat x)|/(2|f'(\hat x)|)\). If \(f''(\hat x)=0\), the convergence is even faster. Therefore, I proved

Theorem 6.2. Let \(f\colon[a,b]\longrightarrow \R\) be a twice continuously differentiable function, and let \(\hat x\in(a,b)\) be its root such that \(f'(\hat x)\neq 0\). Then there exists an interval \(I=[\hat x-\delta,\hat x+\delta]\subset[a,b]\), \(\delta>0\), such that for any \(x_0\in I\) the Newton–Raphson method converges to \(\hat x\), and its order of convergence is at least two (exactly two with the rate of convergence \(\mu=|f''(\hat x)|/(2|f'(\hat x)|)\) if \(f''(\hat x)\neq 0\)).

Moreover, \(\delta\) can be any positive number such that \(I\subset[a,b]\) and \(M\delta<1\), where \[ M=\frac{\max_{x\in I}|f''(x)|}{2\min_{x\in I}|f'(x)|}\,. \]

(The constant \(M\) in the theorem is a bound for \(|f''(y)/(2f'(x))|\), \(x,y\in I\), as in the proof above; in the theorem I assume that \(f'\neq 0\) on \(I\).)

Let me check the theorem numerically for my favorite test equation \(x^2-2=0\), for which \[ \mu=\frac{|f''(\sqrt 2)|}{2|f'(\sqrt 2)|}=\frac{2}{4\sqrt 2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt 2}\approx 0.354,\quad \log\mu\approx -1.040. \] I use the same approach as for the bisection method in Section 4.5: I compute the errors, print them together with the ratios \(\varepsilon_{n}/\varepsilon_{n-1}^2\), and find the best fitting straight line for the points \((\log\varepsilon_n,\log\varepsilon_{n+1})\), whose slope should be close to the order \(q=2\).

import math
import numpy as np


def f(x):
    return x * x - 2


def df(x):
    return 2 * x


def newton_errors(f, df, x0, N, root):
    """Perform N steps of the Newton-Raphson method for the function f with
    the derivative df, starting from x0, and return the list of the absolute
    errors |x_n - root| for n = 0, 1, ..., N."""
    x = x0
    errors = [abs(x - root)]
    for n in range(N):
        x = x - f(x) / df(x)
        errors.append(abs(x - root))
    return errors


errors = newton_errors(f, df, 2.0, 4, math.sqrt(2))

print(f"n = 0   error = {errors[0]:.2e}")
for n in range(1, len(errors)):
    ratio = errors[n] / errors[n - 1]**2
    print(f"n = {n}   error = {errors[n]:.2e}   error_n / error_(n-1)^2 = {ratio:.4f}")

log_errors = [math.log(e) for e in errors]
slope, shift = np.polyfit(log_errors[:-1], log_errors[1:], 1)
print(f"slope = {slope}")
print(f"shift = {shift}")
n = 0   error = 5.86e-01
n = 1   error = 8.58e-02   error_n / error_(n-1)^2 = 0.2500
n = 2   error = 2.45e-03   error_n / error_(n-1)^2 = 0.3333
n = 3   error = 2.12e-06   error_n / error_(n-1)^2 = 0.3529
n = 4   error = 1.59e-12   error_n / error_(n-1)^2 = 0.3535
slope = 1.9791502216834844
shift = -1.2565462449178268

Here I take only \(N=4\) steps: already the next iterate coincides with the float number closest to \(\sqrt 2\), its error is 0.0, and the logarithm of zero cannot be computed. The number of correct digits indeed approximately doubles at each step, the ratios approach \(\mu\approx 0.354\), and the slope is very close to 2. The shift is not that close to \(\log\mu\approx-1.040\), because the first errors are not small, and for them \(\varepsilon_{n+1}\approx\mu\varepsilon_n^2\) is not yet a good approximation (look at the first ratio); with only four points, they noticeably affect the fit.

Remark 6.3. The order of convergence two is usually interpreted as “the number of correct digits in the answer is approximately doubled at each iteration.” While this certainly can be true for many examples, statements like this should always be taken with a grain of salt. It is possible to come up with particular equations where this rule (doubling the number of correct digits at each step) does not hold, at least during the first steps. Take, e.g., the equation \[ f(x)=e^x-x-1.000000005=0 \] starting with \(x_0=0.0005\) and analyze it numerically (see Exercise 6.2).

Example 6.4. Let \[ f(x)=x^3+x-2, \] and assume that I need to find the root \(\hat x=1\) using the Newton–Raphson method. I check directly that the interval \([0,2]\) brackets my root. I also have \[ \min_{x\in[0,2]}|f'(x)|=\min_{x\in[0,2]}|3 x^2+1|=1, \] and \[ \max_{x\in[0,2]} |f''(x)|=\max_{x\in[0,2]}|6x|=12. \] For any interval \(I\subset[0,2]\) the maximum of \(|f''|\) on \(I\) is not bigger than 12, and the minimum of \(|f'|\) on \(I\) is not smaller than 1, hence I can choose the constant \(M=12/(2\cdot 1)=6\). Since I must have \(M\delta<1\), I see that taking \(\delta<1/6\) will result in the required inequality, and hence I showed that for any \[ x_0\in(5/6,7/6) \] the Newton–Raphson method will converge.

Of course, the analysis in this example is somewhat artificial, since for a real task we would never know what the root is exactly.

Theorem 6.2 gives quite a nice and detailed description of the local properties of the Newton–Raphson method. Can we supplement it with any discussion of global properties? In some cases, yes. For instance, if \(f'(x)>0\) and \(f''(x)>0\) for all \(x\in[\hat x, c]\), then for any initial point \(x_0\in(\hat x, c]\) the Newton–Raphson method will converge to \(\hat x\). Similarly, if \(f'(x)>0\) and \(f''(x)<0\) for all \(x\in[c,\hat x]\), then for any \(x_0\in[c,\hat x)\) the iterates will approach \(\hat x\) (see Exercise 6.1). In general, however, I cannot make such bold conclusions, and the situation may become very complicated, as the following example shows.

Example 6.5. Let \(f(x)=x^3-x\); the equation \(f(x)=0\) has three roots \(\hat x_0=0\), \(\hat x_{\pm 1}=\pm 1\). The Newton–Raphson method is the simple iteration \(x_{n+1}=N(x_n)\) with \[ N(x)=x-\frac{x^3-x}{3x^2-1}=\frac{2x^3}{3x^2-1}\,, \] which is not defined at the points \(\pm 1/\sqrt3\), where \(f'(x)=3x^2-1=0\). Since \(f'(\hat x)\neq 0\) at all three roots, Theorem 6.2 says that the method converges if \(x_0\) is close enough to any of them. But which initial points lead to which root?

Let me define the basin of attraction \(B(\hat x)\) of the root (fixed point of \(N\)) \(\hat x\) as the set of all initial points for which the iterates converge to \(\hat x\): \[ B(\hat x)=\{x_0\colon x_n\to \hat x,\,x_{n+1}=N(x_n)\}\,. \]

Some parts of the picture are simple.

  • If \(x_0\geq 1\), then \(f'(x)>0\) and \(f''(x)=6x>0\) on \([1,x_0]\), and the iterates converge to \(1\) (Exercise 6.1). If \(x_0\in(1/\sqrt3,1)\), then \[ N(x_0)-1=\frac{(x_0-1)^2(2x_0+1)}{3x_0^2-1}>0, \] that is, already \(x_1>1\), and again the iterates converge to \(1\). Hence \((1/\sqrt3,\infty)\subset B(1)\), and, by symmetry (\(N\) is an odd function), \((-\infty,-1/\sqrt3)\subset B(-1)\).

  • If \(|x_0|<1/\sqrt5\), then \(|N(x)|/|x|=2x^2/(1-3x^2)<1\), and the iterates converge to \(0\). Note that \(f''(0)=0\), and indeed the convergence to \(0\) is faster than quadratic: \(N(x)\approx -2x^3\) for small \(x\). Hence \((-1/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5)\subset B(0)\).

  • The points \(\pm1/\sqrt5\) are special: \(N(1/\sqrt5)=-1/\sqrt5\) and \(N(-1/\sqrt5)=1/\sqrt5\), so the iterates jump between these two points forever and do not converge at all.

The interesting part is the interval \(J=(1/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt3)\) (and its mirror image \(-J\)). On \(J\), the function \(N\) is decreasing and maps \(J\) onto \((-\infty,-1/\sqrt5)\). The points of \(J\) that are mapped into \((-\infty,-1/\sqrt3)\) form an interval that belongs to \(B(-1)\). The points of \(J\) that are mapped into \(-J\) form another interval, and on it the whole story repeats, but with the roles of \(1\) and \(-1\) interchanged: a part of it belongs to \(B(1)\), and the rest is mapped into \(J\) again, etc. As a result, \(J\) consists of infinitely many intervals, which belong alternately to \(B(-1)\) and to \(B(1)\), and which accumulate at \(1/\sqrt5\), their lengths quickly approaching zero. This is clearly visible in Fig. 6.1, which I obtained by running the Newton–Raphson method from many initial points. For example, \(x_0=0.45\) and \(x_0=0.5\) lead to \(-1\), whereas \(x_0=0.46\), which lies between them, leads to \(+1\): starting close to \(\hat x=1\) does not at all guarantee that the iterates converge to it.

A similar picture can happen for any iterative method, and in general there is little hope to understand the global behavior of the iterates. (If you are curious, look up the pictures of the basins of attraction of the Newton–Raphson method for polynomial equations in the complex plane: they are fractals.)

6.2Implementation

Now I am ready to turn the Newton–Raphson method into a function, similar to the function bisection from Section 3. The main question, as for bisection, is when to stop. Unlike bisection, the Newton–Raphson method does not provide an interval that contains the root, but quadratic convergence suggests a simple stopping criterion. By \(\eqref{eq6:2}\), close to the root the error of \(x_{n+1}\) is much smaller than the error of \(x_n\), hence \[ x_n-x_{n+1}=(x_n-\hat x)-(x_{n+1}-\hat x)\approx x_n-\hat x. \] That is, the step \(|x_{n+1}-x_n|\) is a good estimate of the error of \(x_n\), and the error of \(x_{n+1}\) is even smaller (approximately \(\mu|x_{n+1}-x_n|^2\)). So I stop when two consecutive iterates differ by less than a given tolerance and return the last iterate. As for bisection, I also set a limit on the number of iterations, and, in addition, I check that I do not divide by zero.

def newton(f, df, x0, tol=1e-12, max_iterations=50):
    """Find a root of f by the Newton-Raphson method, starting from x0.

    df is the derivative of f. The iterations stop when two consecutive
    iterates differ by less than tol, and the last iterate is returned.
    If this does not happen after max_iterations steps, or if the derivative
    becomes zero, an error is raised.
    """
    x = x0
    for k in range(max_iterations):
        dfx = df(x)
        if dfx == 0:
            raise ZeroDivisionError(f"The derivative is zero at x = {x}.")
        x_new = x - f(x) / dfx
        if abs(x_new - x) < tol:
            return x_new
        x = x_new

    raise RuntimeError(f"No convergence; the last iterate is {x}.")

Here ZeroDivisionError is the kind of error that Python itself reports when one divides by zero (recall Section 3). The default tolerance \(10^{-12}\) is chosen with the floating-point numbers in mind: for a root of the order of 1, the distance between neighboring float numbers is about \(2\cdot10^{-16}\), so a much smaller tolerance cannot be reached.

Let me test the function. With the same functions f and df as above, and the function from Section 3 for the equation \(\tan x=-2x\),

def f2(x):
    return math.tan(x) + 2 * x


def df2(x):
    return 1 / math.cos(x)**2 + 2

I get

>>> newton(f, df, 2.0)
1.414213562373095
>>> newton(f2, df2, 2.0)
1.8365972031521258
>>> newton(f2, df2, 2.5)
0.0
>>> newton(f, df, 2.0, tol=1e-17)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
RuntimeError                              Traceback (most recent call last)
...
RuntimeError: No convergence; the last iterate is 1.414213562373095.

Several things are worth noting here.

  • The answer for \(\sqrt2\) differs from math.sqrt(2), which is 1.4142135623730951, in the last digit. As I noted above, already \(x_5\) is exactly math.sqrt(2); the iterations continued because the step from \(x_4\) to \(x_5\) was larger than the tolerance, and \(x_6\) differs from \(x_5\) by one neighboring float number because of rounding errors.

  • For \(\mu_1\), starting from \(x_0=2\), I get all the digits of the correct value \(1.8365972031521257227\ldots\) that a float number can hold; bisection gave only 8 correct digits with its default tolerance.

  • Starting from \(x_0=2.5\), which is still in the interval \((\pi/2,\pi)\), the method converges to the root \(x=0\) of \(\tan x+2x=0\), not to \(\mu_1\). There is no bracketing interval here, and the method can converge to any root, see Example 6.5. Always check the answer!

  • As for bisection, a tolerance that is too small cannot be reached, and the function raises an error.

6.3Exercises

Exercise 6.1. Prove that if \(f'(x)>0\) and \(f''(x)>0\) for all \(x\in[\hat x, c]\), where \(f(\hat x)=0\), then for any initial point \(x_0\in(\hat x, c]\) the Newton–Raphson method converges to \(\hat x\). (Hint: show that the iterates form a decreasing sequence bounded below by \(\hat x\).) Formulate and prove a similar statement for the case \(f'(x)>0\) and \(f''(x)<0\) for all \(x\in[c,\hat x]\).

Exercise 6.2. Apply the Newton–Raphson method to the equation \(e^x-x-1.000000005=0\) starting with \(x_0=0.0005\). With float numbers you will get only about 8 correct significant digits of the root, which is of the order \(10^{-4}\) (can you explain why? Hint: look at the computation of \(e^x-x-1.000000005\) for small \(x\)), so use the package mpmath (see Section 3), which allows one to compute with any prescribed number of digits. How many correct digits do you gain at each step? Explain your observations using Theorem 6.2 (a more advanced part of the exercise). (Hint: what is \(f'(\hat x)\), and what is \(M\varepsilon_0\) for this equation?)
