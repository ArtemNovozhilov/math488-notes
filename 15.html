15.1Free nodes

In the previous section I approximated \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx\sum_{j=0}^n w_jf(x_j) \] by choosing \(n+1\) equally spaced points \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\) and computing the weights \(w_0,\ldots,w_n\). Since the weights are my only free parameters, there are \(n+1\) of them, and I can make the formula exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(n\), which also has \(n+1\) coefficients (for even \(n\) I got one more degree as a bonus because of the symmetry).

Now let me ask a different question: what if I am allowed to choose not only the weights but also the points \(x_j\)? Then I have \(2n+2\) free parameters, and I can hope to make my formula exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(2n+1\), since such a polynomial has exactly \(2n+2\) coefficients. The resulting formulas are called Gaussian quadratures. Below I will work on the interval \([-1,1]\), and at the end I will move to an arbitrary interval \([a,b]\) using the linear change of variables from the previous section.

15.2Gaussian quadratures with two and three points

Let me start with the method of undetermined coefficients.

Example 15.1 (Two points). I am looking for the formula \[ \int_{-1}^1f(x)\D x\approx w_0f(x_0)+w_1f(x_1), \] which is exact for \(1,x,x^2,x^3\). Since the interval \([-1,1]\) is symmetric with respect to zero, it is natural to look for symmetric points \(x_0=-t\), \(x_1=t\) with \(t>0\), and equal weights \(w_0=w_1=w\). Then for any odd function the formula gives zero, as the integral itself, and hence the formula is automatically exact for \(x\) and \(x^3\). For \(1\) and \(x^2\) I have \[ 2=\int_{-1}^1 1\D x=2w,\quad \frac{2}{3}=\int_{-1}^1 x^2\D x=2wt^2, \] and hence \(w=1\), \(t=1/\sqrt{3}\). My first Gaussian quadrature is \begin{equation}\label{eq15:1} \int_{-1}^1f(x)\D x\approx f\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt 3}\right)+f\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt 3}\right), \end{equation} which uses only two values of \(f\), and yet is exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(3\), exactly as Simpson’s rule, which needs three values.

Example 15.2 (Three points). Now I take three points \(-t,0,t\) with the weights \(w_0\), \(w_1\), \(w_0\), respectively. Again, the formula is automatically exact for any odd function, and I need to make it exact for \(1,x^2,x^4\): \[ 2w_0+w_1=2,\quad 2w_0t^2=\frac 23\,,\quad 2w_0t^4=\frac{2}{5}\,. \] Dividing the third equation by the second one, I find \(t^2=3/5\), and then \(w_0=5/9\), \(w_1=8/9\), i.e., \begin{equation}\label{eq15:2} \int_{-1}^1f(x)\D x\approx \frac{5}{9}f\left(-\sqrt{\frac 35}\right)+\frac{8}{9}f(0)+\frac{5}{9}f\left(\sqrt{\frac 35}\right), \end{equation} which is exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(5\).

For larger \(n\) the equations for the weights and points become nonlinear and quite messy, and therefore I need a better approach.

15.3The general construction

Here is the key observation. Let \[ \pi_{n+1}(x)=(x-x_0)(x-x_1)\ldots(x-x_n) \] be the polynomial of degree \(n+1\) that vanishes at my points (as in Section 13). If my formula is exact for all polynomials of degree at most \(2n+1\), then, in particular, it is exact for \(\pi_{n+1}(x)x^k\), \(k=0,\ldots,n\), and since \(\pi_{n+1}(x_j)=0\) for all \(j\), I must have \begin{equation}\label{eq15:3} \int_{-1}^1\pi_{n+1}(x)x^k\D x=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,n. \end{equation} Since \(\pi_{n+1}(x)=x^{n+1}+c_nx^n+\ldots+c_1x+c_0\), conditions \(\eqref{eq15:3}\) form a system of \(n+1\) linear equations for the \(n+1\) unknown coefficients \(c_0,\ldots,c_n\); the element of its matrix in row \(k\) and column \(i\) is \(\int_{-1}^1x^{i+k}\D x\). It can be shown that this system always has a unique solution. Note that if I worked on \([0,1]\) instead of \([-1,1]\), these elements would be \(\int_0^1x^{i+k}\D x=1/(i+k+1)\), i.e., the matrix of the system would be exactly the Hilbert matrix \(\bs H_{n+1}\) from Section 11. Hence solving this system numerically is not a good way to find the points for large \(n\) (see Exercise 15.4); I will use it only for the theory.

Example 15.3. Let \(n=1\), i.e., \(\pi_2(x)=x^2+c_1x+c_0\). Conditions \(\eqref{eq15:3}\) give \[ \int_{-1}^1(x^2+c_1x+c_0)\D x=\frac 23+2c_0=0,\quad \int_{-1}^1(x^3+c_1x^2+c_0x)\D x=\frac{2c_1}{3}=0, \] hence \(\pi_2(x)=x^2-\frac 13\), whose roots are \(\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt 3}\), exactly the points in \(\eqref{eq15:1}\).

To use the roots of \(\pi_{n+1}\) as my points, I need to make sure that they are real, distinct, and belong to the interval of integration.

Lemma 15.4. Let \(\pi_{n+1}\) be a polynomial of degree \(n+1\) satisfying \(\eqref{eq15:3}\). Then \(\pi_{n+1}\) has \(n+1\) distinct real roots in \((-1,1)\).

Proof. Let \(z_1,\ldots,z_m\) be the points in \((-1,1)\) at which \(\pi_{n+1}\) changes its sign, and assume that \(m\leq n\). Then the polynomial \[ \pi_{n+1}(x)(x-z_1)\ldots(x-z_m) \] does not change its sign on \((-1,1)\) and is not identically zero, and hence its integral over \([-1,1]\) is not zero. On the other hand, \((x-z_1)\ldots(x-z_m)\) is a polynomial of degree \(m\leq n\), i.e., a combination of \(1,x,\ldots,x^n\), and by \(\eqref{eq15:3}\) this integral is zero. This contradiction shows that \(m\geq n+1\), i.e., \(\pi_{n+1}\) changes its sign at least \(n+1\) times in \((-1,1)\). Since a polynomial of degree \(n+1\) cannot have more than \(n+1\) roots, all its roots are real, distinct, and lie in \((-1,1)\). ■

Now I take the roots of \(\pi_{n+1}\) as my points \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\), and, exactly as for the Newton–Cotes formulas, I take the weights \begin{equation}\label{eq15:4} w_k=\int_{-1}^1L_k(x)\D x,\quad k=0,\ldots,n, \end{equation} where \(L_k\) are the polynomials from the Lagrange interpolation polynomial. Recall that with these weights the formula is exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(n\).

Theorem 15.5. Let \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\) be the roots of \(\pi_{n+1}\) satisfying \(\eqref{eq15:3}\), and let the weights be given by \(\eqref{eq15:4}\). Then the formula \[ \int_{-1}^1f(x)\D x\approx\sum_{k=0}^nw_kf(x_k) \] is exact for any polynomial of degree at most \(2n+1\). Moreover, no formula with \(n+1\) points is exact for all polynomials of degree \(2n+2\).

Proof. Let \(p\) be a polynomial of degree at most \(2n+1\). I divide it by \(\pi_{n+1}\) with remainder: \[ p(x)=q(x)\pi_{n+1}(x)+r(x), \] where \(q\) and \(r\) are polynomials of degree at most \(n\). For the exact integral I have, by \(\eqref{eq15:3}\), \[ \int_{-1}^1p(x)\D x=\int_{-1}^1q(x)\pi_{n+1}(x)\D x+\int_{-1}^1r(x)\D x=\int_{-1}^1r(x)\D x. \] For my formula I have, since \(\pi_{n+1}(x_k)=0\), \[ \sum_{k=0}^nw_kp(x_k)=\sum_{k=0}^nw_kq(x_k)\pi_{n+1}(x_k)+\sum_{k=0}^nw_kr(x_k)=\sum_{k=0}^nw_kr(x_k). \] Finally, \(r\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\), for which my formula is exact, and hence the right-hand sides of the last two equalities coincide.

For the second statement, take any points \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\) and consider \(p(x)=(x-x_0)^2\ldots(x-x_n)^2\) of degree \(2n+2\). Any formula with these points gives zero for \(p\), whereas \[ \int_{-1}^1p(x)\D x>0. \] ■

Remark 15.6. The weights of a Gaussian quadrature are always positive. Indeed, \(L_k^2\) is a polynomial of degree \(2n\), for which the formula is exact, and \(L_k(x_j)=0\) for \(j\neq k\), \(L_k(x_k)=1\). Hence \[ 0<\int_{-1}^1L_k^2(x)\D x=\sum_{j=0}^nw_jL_k^2(x_j)=w_k. \] This is an important difference from the Newton–Cotes formulas, whose weights become negative for large \(n\) (in Section 14 this happened already for \(n=8\)), which makes them sensitive to rounding errors.

Remark 15.7. The polynomials \(\pi_{n+1}\) defined by \(\eqref{eq15:3}\) are, up to a constant factor, the so-called Legendre polynomials, and their roots and the corresponding weights are tabulated and available in standard software packages. I will not need any of their properties beyond what I proved above.

15.4An arbitrary interval and the error

To use a Gaussian quadrature on \([a,b]\), I use the change of variables \(x=\varphi(t)=\frac{b-a}{2}\,t+\frac{a+b}{2}\), which maps \([-1,1]\) onto \([a,b]\) (see Section 14): \[ \int_a^bf(x)\D x=\frac{b-a}{2}\int_{-1}^1f(\varphi(t))\D t\approx\frac{b-a}{2}\sum_{k=0}^nw_kf\left(\frac{b-a}{2}\,t_k+\frac{a+b}{2}\right), \] where \(t_k\) and \(w_k\) are the points and weights on \([-1,1]\).

It can be shown that if \(f\in C^{2n+2}[a,b]\) then the error of the Gaussian quadrature with \(n+1\) points is \[ \frac{(b-a)^{2n+3}\bigl((n+1)!\bigr)^4}{(2n+3)\bigl((2n+2)!\bigr)^3}\,f^{(2n+2)}(\xi) \] for some \(\xi\in(a,b)\). For instance, for two points (\(n=1\)) the error is \(\frac{(b-a)^5}{4320}f^{(4)}(\xi)\), whereas for Simpson’s rule, which uses three points, it is \(\frac{(b-a)^5}{2880}f^{(4)}(\xi)\) in absolute value (recall that \(h=(b-a)/2\) in the estimate \(h^5M_4/90\)).

Example 15.8. Consider again \[ \int_0^1e^x\D x=e-1\approx 1.718281828459045. \] Simpson’s rule with three values of \(f\) gives the error \(5.8\cdot 10^{-4}\). The Gaussian quadrature with two points gives the error \(3.9\cdot 10^{-4}\), and with three points the error is \(8.2\cdot 10^{-7}\), i.e., about 700 times smaller than for Simpson’s rule with the same number of values of \(f\).

15.5Python code

In practice the points and weights on \([-1,1]\) are computed by a library function; for instance, np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(m) returns the arrays of \(m\) points and \(m\) weights (here \(m=n+1\) is the number of points). The sum \(\sum_k w_kf(x_k)\) is the dot product of the array of the weights and the array of the values of \(f\), which is written w @ f(x) (Section 9).

import numpy as np


def gauss(f, a, b, m):
    """The Gaussian quadrature with m points for the integral of f over [a, b]."""
    t, w = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(m)   # the points and weights on [-1, 1]
    x = (b - a) / 2 * t + (a + b) / 2           # the points mapped to [a, b]
    return (b - a) / 2 * (w @ f(x))


exact = np.e - 1
for m in range(1, 6):
    result = gauss(np.exp, 0, 1, m)
    print(f"{m}  {result:.15f}  {abs(result - exact):.1e}")

With only five values of \(f\) I get the integral with an error of order \(10^{-13}\). Compare this with Section 14: the composite trapezoidal rule (the function trapezoid_tol) needed \(262145\) values of \(f\) to get an error of about \(2\cdot 10^{-12}\), and even the composite Simpson’s rule (simpson_tol) needed \(513\) values to get an error of about \(10^{-13}\). Similarly to the Newton–Cotes formulas, for less smooth functions or long intervals one can use composite Gaussian quadratures, dividing \([a,b]\) into subintervals and using a Gaussian quadrature with a small number of points on each of them (Exercise 15.5).

As in the case of polynomial interpolation, where choosing the Chebyshev points instead of equally spaced ones made a huge difference (Section 13), the main lesson here is that the choice of the points matters a lot.

(Hint: \(\int_{-1}^1x^j\D x\) is \(2/(j+1)\) for even \(j\) and \(0\) for odd \(j\).) Compare your points with the ones returned by np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(m) for \(m=2,4,\ldots,20\), and compute the condition numbers of the matrices of your systems. Explain what you see.
