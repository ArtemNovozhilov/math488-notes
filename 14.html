14.1Introductory discussion

Recall that the definite integral of a sufficiently nice function \(f\) on the interval \([a,b]\) is denoted \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x, \] and has the geometric meaning of the signed area between the graph of \(f\) and the \(x\)-axis. The word “signed” means that I take this area with the sign “plus” if \(f(x)>0\) and with the sign “minus” if \(f(x)<0\). In Calculus we usually use the fundamental theorem of calculus to evaluate the definite integral, i.e., \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x=F(b)-F(a), \] where \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\), i.e., \(F'(x)=f(x)\). For instance, \[ \int_0^1 e^x\D x=\left.e^x\right|_{0}^1=e^1-e^0=e-1\approx 1.72, \] since \(e^x\) is an antiderivative of \(e^x\). It is, however, true that there are functions whose antiderivatives cannot be written down if we are allowed to use only the elementary functions: polynomials, rational functions, trig functions, \(\exp\) and \(\log\), and their combinations. The very first problem in these notes (Section 1) was such an example: \[ \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\D x. \] It can be proven (though not in an elementary way) that no antiderivative of \(f(x)=e^{-x^2}\) is an elementary function. Do not get me wrong: all I am saying is that this antiderivative is impossible to write down with our usual supply of functions, not that there is no such thing as the definite integral itself. As a matter of fact, if you recall your Calculus class, you should be able to write this antiderivative down as \[ F(x)=\int_0^x e^{-s^2}\D s, \] which, by the second part of the fundamental theorem of calculus, implies that \[ F'(x)=e^{-x^2}. \] But how to compute \(F\) at any given point \(x\)? For this I need approximate numerical methods. In Section 1 I used the simplest one, the leftpoint rule \(L_n\), and in Section 2 I implemented it as the function leftpoint(f, a, b, n). I also observed there that the error of \(L_n\) decreases as \(1/n\), namely, that \(n\) times the error approaches the number \(0.316\ldots\), and I promised to come back to this. It is my goal in this section to look at such methods from a more theoretical point of view, to explain this number, and to find much better methods.

14.2Newton–Cotes formulas

Again, the goal is to compute \begin{equation}\label{eq14:1} \int_a^b f(x)\D x. \end{equation} If I divide the interval \([a,b]\) into \(n\) subintervals and take the \(n+1\) points \[ x_j=a+jh,\quad h=\frac{b-a}{n}\,,\quad j=0,\ldots, n, \] then I can compute the Lagrange interpolation polynomial of degree at most \(n\) (Section 13), \[ p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n L_k(x)f(x_k), \] which hopefully will be close to my function: \[ p_n(x)\approx f(x),\quad x\in[a,b]. \] (Remember that this is not always true!)

Taking different \(n\) I will get different formulas, specifically, \begin{equation}\label{eq14:2} \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx\int_a^b p_n(x)\D x=\sum_{k=0}^n f(x_k)\int_a^b L_k(x)\D x. \end{equation} Formulas \(\eqref{eq14:2}\) are called the Newton–Cotes formulas of degree \(n\) (many textbooks say “of order \(n\)”; I avoid this, since the word “order” is already used for the speed of convergence, see Sections 4–6, and will be used in this sense below). To be able to use them, I need to compute the weights \[ w_k = \int_a^b L_k(x)\D x,\quad k=0,\ldots,n, \] which depend only on the mesh points \(x_j\). Once computed, they can be used for any given function \(f\) on the interval \([a,b]\): \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx w_0 f_0+w_1 f_1+\ldots +w_{n-1}f_{n-1}+w_n f_n, \] where I use the notation \(f_j=f(x_j)\), which will be convenient below.

Now let me switch from the general discussion to some concrete examples.

Example 14.1 (Trapezoidal rule). Take \(n=1\) for my interpolation polynomial (I cannot take \(n=0\), since I always want to include at least two points \(x_0=a\) and \(x_n=b\), but see below). The geometric meaning of this approximation should be clear: I replace the area under the graph of my function \(f\) with the area of the trapezoid with vertices at \((x_0,0),(x_0,f_0),(x_1,f_1),(x_1,0)\) (make a picture!). Denoting \(h=b-a\), I immediately get that the area of this trapezoid is \(h(f_0+f_1)/2\), which will be my Newton–Cotes formula of degree 1: \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx \frac{h}{2}(f_0+f_1). \] Let me derive the same formula using the Lagrange interpolation polynomial.

I have \[ p_1(x)=f_0\frac{x-x_1}{x_0-x_1}+f_1\frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}=f_0\frac{x-x_1}{-h}+f_1\frac{x-x_0}{h}\,. \] In this case \(x_0=a\) and \(x_1=b\), hence \[ \int_{x_0}^{x_1}p_1(x)\D x=-\frac{f_0}{h}\int_{x_0}^{x_1}(x-x_1)\D x+\frac{f_1}{h}\int_{x_0}^{x_1}(x-x_0)\D x=\frac{h}{2}(f_0+f_1), \] as expected.

Example 14.2 (Simpson’s rule). Now it is time to tackle the case \(n=2\). My points are now \(x_0=a\), \(x_1=(a+b)/2\), \(x_2=b\), and \(h=(b-a)/2\).

To apply this case I need \(w_0,w_1,w_2\). Specifically, \[ w_0=\int_{x_0}^{x_2} L_0(x)\D x=\int_{x_0}^{x_2}\frac{(x-x_1)(x-x_2)}{(x_0-x_1)(x_0-x_2)}\D x=\frac{1}{2h^2}\int_{x_0}^{x_2}(x-x_1)(x-x_2)\D x. \] I could have computed the last integral directly, but instead I will make the substitution \[ x = ht+x_1. \] I get (remember that you need to change the expression under the integral sign, the limits of integration, and \(\D x\)): \[ w_0=\frac{1}{2h^2}\int_{-1}^1 ht(ht-h)h\D t=\frac{h}{2}\int_{-1}^1t(t-1)\D t=\frac{h}{3}\,. \]

Similarly (an exercise) I can find \[ w_1=\frac{4h}{3},\quad w_2=w_0=\frac{h}{3}\,. \]

Summarizing, my Newton–Cotes formula of degree 2 is given by \[ \int_a^bf(x)\D x\approx\frac{h}{3}(f_0+4f_1+f_2), \] which is called Simpson’s rule (and I hope you recognize this formula, since I would guess you saw it in your Calculus class).

Clearly, I can take \(n=3,4,\ldots\), and the list of Newton–Cotes formulas can be continued. For instance, the Newton–Cotes formula of degree \(3\) is (see Exercise 14.1) \[ \int_{x_0}^{x_3}f(x)\D x\approx \frac{3h}{8}(f_0+3f_1+3f_2+f_3). \] I have, however, two other related questions. The first one, of course, is what error I make when I replace my exact integral with an approximate formula. The second question is whether there is a more efficient method to produce Newton–Cotes formulas for different \(n\) and, please note, for possibly not equally spaced \(x_j\). Let me start with the error.

Example 14.3 (The error of the trapezoidal rule). Recall that for the Lagrange interpolation polynomial \(p_n\) on \([a,b]\) I have the following error estimate (Section 13): \[ |f(x)-p_n(x)|\leq \frac{M_{n+1}}{(n+1)!}|\pi_{n+1}(x)|, \] where, as before, \(M_{n+1}\) is the maximum of the absolute value of the \((n+1)\)-st derivative of \(f\) on \([a,b]\), and \[ \pi_{n+1}(x)=(x-x_0)(x-x_1)\ldots(x-x_n) \] is a polynomial of degree \(n+1\). Now, for my specific case \(n=1\), I have, with \(h=b-a\) and the substitution \(x=ht+a\), \begin{align*} \left|\int_a^b f(x)\D x-\int_a^b p_1(x)\D x\right|&=\left|\int_a^b(f(x)-p_1(x))\D x\right|\leq \int_a^b |f(x)-p_1(x)|\D x\\ &\leq \frac{M_2}{2!}\int_a^b|\pi_2(x)|\D x= \frac{M_2}{2!}\int_a^b(x-a)(b-x)\D x\\ &=\frac{M_2}{2!}\int_0^1 ht(h-ht)h\D t=\frac{h^3M_2}{12}=\frac{M_2(b-a)^3}{12}\,. \end{align*} In fact, I can say more. Recall from the proof of the theorem in Section 13 that \(f(x)-p_1(x)=\frac{1}{2}f''(\xi)(x-a)(x-b)\), where \(\xi\) depends on \(x\). Since \((x-a)(x-b)\) does not change its sign on \([a,b]\), the mean value theorem for integrals gives \begin{equation}\label{eq14:3} \int_a^b f(x)\D x-\frac{h}{2}(f_0+f_1)=\frac{f''(\eta)}{2}\int_a^b(x-a)(x-b)\D x=-\frac{h^3}{12}f''(\eta) \end{equation} for some \(\eta\in(a,b)\).

Not surprisingly, in my error estimate for the trapezoidal rule I have the second derivative of the function. Notice that the second derivative of a constant function or of a polynomial of degree one is zero, which implies that the trapezoidal rule is exact for any polynomials \(p_0,p_1\). Clearly, this is something I would expect to see. Moreover, it should be quite clear that the Newton–Cotes formula of degree \(n\) is exact for any polynomial of degree \(n\) or below, simply because of the uniqueness of the Lagrange interpolation polynomial, and hence the error estimate for degree \(n\) must include \(M_{n+1}\) and \(h^{n+2}\). For instance, my expectation for Simpson’s rule is that the error is proportional to \(h^4\). The reality, however, is better than that! Here are some mathematical details that explain it.

Theorem 14.4. Consider a Newton–Cotes formula of degree \(n\): \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx\sum_{j=0}^n w_j f_j. \] Then \[ w_j=w_{n-j}, \] and, moreover, if \(n\) is even, the resulting approximate formula is exact for any polynomial of degree \(n+1\).

Proof. Let \(x_j=a+hj\), \(j=0,\ldots, n\), \(h=(b-a)/n\). I have \(x_{n-j}=a+(n-j)h=b-hj\), and hence \(x_j=a+b-x_{n-j}\).

Now, by definition, \[ w_j=\int_a^b\prod_{i=0,i\neq j}^n\frac{x-x_i}{x_j-x_i}\D x=(x=a+b -y)=-\int_b^a\prod_{i=0,i\neq j}^n\frac{a+b-y-x_i}{x_j-x_i}\D y. \] Using the above expression for \(x_j\) I find \[ w_j=\int_a^b\prod_{i=0,i\neq j}^n \frac{x_{n-i}-y}{x_{n-i}-x_{n-j}}\D y=\int_a^b\prod_{k=0,k\neq n-j}^n\frac{y-x_k}{x_{n-j}-x_k}\D y=w_{n-j}, \] as required (note that I relabeled the index in the product).

Now I know that my formula must be exact for any polynomial of degree \(n\). Assume that \(n\) is even and consider the polynomial \[ q(x)=\left(x-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{n+1}\,. \] This polynomial has odd degree and is antisymmetric with respect to the center of the interval \([a,b]\), meaning that \(q(x_j)=-q(x_{n-j})\) and \(q(x_{n/2})=0\). Therefore, \(\int_a^b q(x)\D x=0\) by antisymmetry, and, since \(w_j=w_{n-j}\), the terms of the sum cancel pairwise: \[ \int_a^b q(x)\D x=0=\sum_{j=0}^n w_jq(x_j), \] i.e., the formula is exact for \(q\). Finally, any polynomial \(p_{n+1}\) of degree \(n+1\) can be represented as \[ p_{n+1}(x)=Aq(x)+r_n(x), \] where \(A\) is some constant and \(r_n\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\); therefore, the Newton–Cotes formula is exact for any polynomial of degree \(n+1\). ■

The discussion above implies that Simpson’s rule is exact for any polynomial of degree \(3\) and below, and hence its error can be estimated as \[ \left|\int_a^b(f(x)-p_3(x))\D x\right|\leq \frac{M_{4}}{4!}\int_a^b|\pi_{4}(x)|\D x; \] the only problem here is that in \(\pi_4\) I must have 4 points, but Simpson’s rule gives me only three: \(a,(a+b)/2,b\). Since I am free to choose the last point myself, I take it to make the error minimal; specifically, I will take \((a+b)/2\) for the second time (strictly speaking, the error estimate from Section 13 was proved for distinct points; one can show that it remains valid if a point is repeated, which corresponds to the so-called Hermite interpolation), which means \[ \pi_4(x)=(x-a)\left(x-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2(x-b)\,. \] Since \[ \int_a^b(x-a)\left(x-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2(b-x)\D x=(x=ht+(a+b)/2)=h^5 \int_{-1}^1(t+1)t^2(1-t)\D t=\frac{4h^5}{15}\,, \] I finally get my estimate for the error in Simpson’s rule: \[ \left|\int_a^bf(x)\D x-w_0f_0-w_1f_1-w_2f_2\right|\leq \frac{h^5M_4}{90}\,, \] i.e., the error is of order 5 with respect to the step \(h=(b-a)/2\).

14.3The method of undetermined coefficients

Is there an easier way to find the approximate formulas? The answer is yes; let me show it by an example.

Example 14.5 (Simpson’s rule again). Let \([a,b]=[0,1]\), and I would like to find the weights to approximate the integral \[ \int_0^1f(x)\D x\approx w_0f_0+w_1f_1+w_2f_2. \] In my case I have \(x_0=0,x_1=0.5,x_2=1\).

Since I already know that my formula must be exact for any polynomial of degree \(2\) and below, it must be exact for the specific examples \(1,x,x^2\). Certainly, I can also compute the integrals of these polynomials, getting \begin{align*} 1 & =\int_0^1 1\D x = w_0+w_1+w_2, \\ \frac{1}{2} & =\int_0^1 x\D x=\frac 12 w_1+ w_2, \\ \frac 13 & = \int_0^1 x^2\D x=\frac 14 w_1+w_2, \end{align*} which is a system of three linear equations with respect to three unknown weights. Solving it, I find \(w_0=w_2=\frac 16,\,w_1=\frac 23\), and hence \[ \int_0^1f(x)\D x \approx \frac 16\left(f_0+4f_1+f_2\right), \] which is almost our general Simpson’s rule, because all the calculations were done for the specific interval \([0,1]\). Now I would like to change it to an arbitrary interval \([a,b]\). For this task consider a slightly more general problem: how to find a linear change of variables that maps the interval \([c,d]\) (say, of variable \(t\)) onto the interval \([a,b]\) of variable \(x\)? Since \[ x =\varphi(t)= At+ B, \] and the ends of the interval must be mapped into the corresponding ends, I must have \[ b = A d+B,\quad a=Ac+B, \] which gives me the solution \[ A =\frac{b-a}{d-c}\,,\quad B=\frac{ad-bc}{d-c}\,, \] which allows me to generalize the result I obtained for the interval \([0,1]\) to an arbitrary \([a,b]\). Namely, \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x=(x=\varphi(t))=\frac{b-a}{d-c}\int_c^df(\varphi(t))\D t\approx\frac{b-a}{d-c}\sum_{i=0}^n w_i f(\varphi(t_i)), \] where \(w_i\) were already computed. In my case, \(c=0\), \(d=1\), and hence \[ \int_a^bf(x)\D x= (b-a)\int_0^1 f((b-a)t+a)\D t\approx(b-a)\bigl(w_0f(a)+w_1f((a+b)/2)+w_2f(b)\bigr), \] and using the weights I found above I recover the general Simpson’s rule.

For larger \(n\) I let the computer solve the system. The conditions that the formula with the points \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\) on \([0,1]\) is exact for \(1,x,\ldots,x^n\) read \[ \sum_{j=0}^n w_jx_j^k=\int_0^1x^k\D x=\frac{1}{k+1}\,,\quad k=0,\ldots,n. \] The matrix of this system has the elements \(x_j^k\) in row \(k\) and column \(j\); this is the transposed Vandermonde matrix from Section 13 (with the powers in the increasing order). In NumPy, np.vander(x, increasing=True) gives the Vandermonde matrix with the columns \(1,x,\ldots,x^n\), and V.T is the transpose of the matrix V.

import numpy as np


def newton_cotes_weights(n):
    """Return the weights of the Newton-Cotes formula of degree n on [0, 1]."""
    x = np.linspace(0, 1, n + 1)            # n + 1 equally spaced points
    V = np.vander(x, increasing=True)       # V[j, k] = x_j^k
    moments = 1 / np.arange(1, n + 2)       # the integrals of x^k, k = 0, ..., n
    return np.linalg.solve(V.T, moments)


for n in [1, 2, 3, 4]:
    print(n, newton_cotes_weights(n))

w = newton_cotes_weights(8)
print(f"n = 8: the smallest weight is {w.min():.4f}")

For \(n=1\) and \(n=2\) I recover the trapezoidal rule and Simpson’s rule (with \(h=1\) and \(h=1/2\), respectively), and for \(n=3\) the formula of degree 3 above with \(h=1/3\). Note, however, that for \(n=8\) some weights are negative. Negative weights are bad news: the formula then subtracts large terms from each other, and the rounding errors in the values \(f_j\) get amplified. This is one more reason not to use Newton–Cotes formulas of high degree.

Similarly to what I have done above, I can find Newton–Cotes formulas for any points, not necessarily equally spaced. For example, there are so-called open Newton–Cotes formulas of degree \(n\) that use the step size \(h=(b-a)/(n+2)\) and disregard the points \(x_0=a\) and \(x_{n+2}=b\). For instance, for \(n=0\) one recovers the familiar midpoint rule \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx (b-a)f((a+b)/2)\,. \]

14.4Composite Newton–Cotes formulas

In the discussion above I used, as examples, only the formulas of degree 1 (trapezoidal rule) and 2 (Simpson’s rule). I only mentioned the formula of degree 3, and never discussed the formulas of degree 4 and above. The reason for this is that it is quite possible to get a large error by using Lagrange interpolation polynomials of high degree (recall the discussion of interpolating the function \(1/(1+x^2)\) in Section 13; also recall the negative weights above). What is usually done in practice is not an increase of the degree, but division of the whole interval \([a,b]\) into smaller intervals and using the formulas obtained above for each interval separately.

Example 14.6 (Composite trapezoidal rule). The goal is to compute, as before, \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x. \] Let \(N\) be the number of intervals I divide \([a,b]\) into; hence I have \(N+1\) points in total, and the length of each interval is \[ h=\frac{b-a}{N}\,. \] Now I apply the trapezoidal rule first on the interval \([x_0,x_1]\): \[ T_1=\frac{h}{2}(f_0+f_1), \] then on the interval \([x_1,x_2]\): \[ T_2=\frac{h}{2}(f_1+f_2), \] and so on, until I get to the final interval, \(T_N=\frac{h}{2}(f_{N-1}+f_N)\).

Now I consider the sum \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx \sum_{j=1}^N T_j=h\left(\frac{f_0+f_N}{2}+f_1+\ldots+f_{N-1}\right)=T(N), \] since each data point \(f_j\) is present twice for all \(j\neq 0,N\). Since on each interval I make an error that does not exceed \(M_2h^3/12=M_2(b-a)^3/(12N^3)\), and there are \(N\) intervals, my total error is \[ \left|\int_a^bf(x)\D x-T(N)\right|=\mathcal O \left(\frac{(b-a)^3M_2}{N^2}\right)=\mathcal O\left(\frac{1}{N^2}\right), \] i.e., the composite trapezoidal rule has order \(2\) with respect to \(h\).

Example 14.7 (Composite Simpson’s rule). Similarly to the above, I can divide my interval \([a,b]\) into \(N=2k\) intervals (this is needed since Simpson’s rule is applied to pairs of adjacent intervals; for an odd number of intervals one can, e.g., use the formula of degree 3 on the last three intervals) and apply Simpson’s rule to pairs of adjacent intervals, i.e., \[ S_1=\frac{h}{3}(f_0+4 f_1+f_2),\quad S_2=\frac{h}{3}(f_2+4f_3+f_4),\quad \ldots,\quad S_{N/2}=\frac{h}{3}(f_{N-2}+4 f_{N-1}+f_N), \] which, after some simplification, gives \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x\approx S(N)=\frac{h}{3}(f_0+4f_1+2f_2+4f_3+2f_4+\ldots+4f_{N-1}+f_N). \] Since for each pair of intervals the error has order 5, the total error for Simpson’s method can be estimated as \[ \left|\int_a^bf(x)\D x-S(N)\right|=\mathcal O \left(\frac{(b-a)^5M_4}{N^4}\right)=\mathcal O\left(\frac{1}{N^4}\right), \] i.e., the composite Simpson’s rule has order \(4\) with respect to \(h\); this is confirmed numerically in the next subsection.

The analysis above indicates that for a sufficiently smooth function \(f\) composite formulas can be made as precise as required, provided that a sufficiently small step \(h\) is chosen (but please remember the rounding errors, whose influence grows with \(N\), recall Section 10).

14.5Back to Section 1

Now I can finally return to the problem from Section 1. Compare the leftpoint rule with the composite trapezoidal rule with the same \(n\): \[ L_n=h\bigl(f_0+f_1+\ldots+f_{n-1}\bigr),\quad T(n)=h\left(\frac{f_0+f_n}{2}+f_1+\ldots+f_{n-1}\right), \] hence \[ T(n)=L_n+\frac{h}{2}\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr). \] The trapezoidal rule uses exactly the same values of \(f\) (plus one), and differs from the leftpoint rule by a simple correction term. Since the error of \(T(n)\) is \(\mathcal O(h^2)\), I get \[ L_n-\int_a^bf(x)\D x=-\frac{h}{2}\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)+\mathcal O(h^2), \] and therefore, with \(h=(b-a)/n\), \[ n\left|L_n-\int_a^bf(x)\D x\right|\to \frac{(b-a)|f(b)-f(a)|}{2}\,,\quad n\to\infty. \] For \(f(x)=e^{-x^2}\) on \([0,1]\) this limit is \((1-e^{-1})/2=0.31606\ldots\), which is exactly the number observed in Section 1.

Similarly, I can find the limit of \(n^2\) times the error of the composite trapezoidal rule. Adding up the exact errors \(\eqref{eq14:3}\) on all the intervals, I get \[ T(n)-\int_a^bf(x)\D x=\frac{h^2}{12}\sum_{j=1}^n hf''(\eta_j)\approx\frac{h^2}{12}\int_a^bf''(x)\D x=\frac{h^2}{12}\bigl(f'(b)-f'(a)\bigr), \] since the sum is a Riemann sum of \(f''\). For \(f(x)=e^{-x^2}\) I have \(f'(x)=-2xe^{-x^2}\), and hence \(n^2\) times the error of \(T(n)\) should approach \(2e^{-1}/12=1/(6e)=0.06131\ldots\) in absolute value.

Let me check all this numerically. In Section 2 I wrote the function leftpoint with a loop and math.exp. With NumPy all three composite rules are written without loops: I compute all the points at once, evaluate \(f\) at all of them at once (for this, \(f\) must use np.exp, not math.exp), and sum. In simpson, the slice y[1:-1:2] contains the values \(f_1,f_3,\ldots,f_{N-1}\), which are multiplied by 4, and y[2:-1:2] contains \(f_2,f_4,\ldots,f_{N-2}\), which are multiplied by 2 (recall the slicing of lists in Section 2). The exact value of the integral is computed as in Section 1.

import math


def f(x):
    return np.exp(-x**2)


def leftpoint(f, a, b, n):
    """The leftpoint rule with n intervals (Sections 1 and 2)."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    x = a + h * np.arange(n)            # the points x_0, ..., x_{n-1}
    return h * np.sum(f(x))


def trapezoid(f, a, b, n):
    """The composite trapezoidal rule with n intervals."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)        # the points x_0, ..., x_n
    y = f(x)
    return h * (np.sum(y) - (y[0] + y[-1]) / 2)


def simpson(f, a, b, n):
    """The composite Simpson's rule with n intervals; n must be even."""
    if n % 2 != 0:
        raise ValueError("The number of intervals must be even.")
    h = (b - a) / n
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)
    y = f(x)
    return h / 3 * (y[0] + 4 * np.sum(y[1:-1:2]) + 2 * np.sum(y[2:-1:2]) + y[-1])


I = math.sqrt(math.pi) / 2 * math.erf(1)       # the exact value of the integral

    print(f"{n:5d}  {error_L:10.2e}  {n * error_L:8.5f}  {error_T:10.2e}  "

14.6Implementation

For my next test function I will take the very simple \[ f(x)=e^x \] on the interval \([0,1]\), for which \[ \int_0^1e^x\D x=e-1\approx 1.718281828459045. \] In the previous subsection I wrote the composite rules in vectorized form. To see what this gives, let me also write the composite trapezoidal rule straightforwardly, with a loop, as I would have done with lists, and compare the two for a large number of intervals (as in Section 9).

import time


def trapezoid_loop(f, a, b, n):
    """The composite trapezoidal rule with n intervals, with a loop over the points."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    total = (f(a) + f(b)) / 2
    for j in range(1, n):
        total += f(a + j * h)
    return h * total


exact = np.e - 1
n = 10**6
for rule in [trapezoid_loop, trapezoid]:
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    result = rule(np.exp, 0, 1, n)
    t1 = time.perf_counter()
    print(f"{rule.__name__:>14}: {result:.15f}, error {abs(result - exact):.1e}, "
          f"{t1 - t0:.4f} seconds")
trapezoid_loop: 1.718281828459131, error 8.6e-14, 0.2060 seconds
     trapezoid: 1.718281828459188, error 1.4e-13, 0.0078 seconds

The results differ only in the last digits: the error of the method for \(n=10^6\) is \(\frac{h^2}{12}(e-1)\approx 1.4\cdot 10^{-13}\) (Section 14.5), and the rounding errors in the sum of \(10^6\) terms, which is computed in a different order in the two functions, are of comparable size. The vectorized version is more than ten times faster. The composite trapezoidal rule is also available in NumPy as np.trapezoid(y, dx=h), where y is the array of the values \(f_0,\ldots,f_N\) (in older versions of NumPy it was called np.trapz, which does not exist anymore).

Both functions, however, have a major drawback: they require the number of intervals as an input parameter. How do I know which \(n\) to submit? I do have some theoretical estimates, but they are usually difficult to apply in concrete cases; moreover, they give very conservative estimates of the error, and my goal is to minimize the number of evaluations of my function. The basic idea, of course, is to compute my integral for two different \(n\), and if the results are sufficiently close to each other, stop the computations. Here is how it can be done rather efficiently.

Let me consider a sequence of approximations for the same interval \([a,b]\), which I will index by the variable \(K\), such that at the \(K\)-th step I have \(N=2^{K-1}\) intervals. For \(K=1\) I have one interval, and my approximation is \[ T(1)=h_1\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}\,,\quad h_1={b-a}. \] For \(K=2\) I have 2 intervals, \(h_2=(b-a)/2\), and \[ T(2)=h_2\left(\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}+f((a+b)/2)\right)=\frac{T(1)}{2}+h_2f((a+b)/2). \] One more step, \(K=3\), \(N=4\), \(h_3=(b-a)/4\): \[ T(3)=h_3\left(\frac{f_0+f_4}{2}+f_2+f_1+f_3\right)=\frac{T(2)}{2}+h_3(f_1+f_3). \] (In this notation, \(T(K)\) is the composite trapezoidal rule with \(2^{K-1}\) intervals.) Now the idea should be clear: at each next step, to compute \(T(K)\) I can use the precomputed value \(T(K-1)\) and the values of the function \(f\) at the “newly added” points \(x_j=a+jh_K\) with odd \(j\), whose number is exactly \(2^{K-2}\). This will allow me to save a lot of time by reusing the results of previous computations. Here is my function that returns \(T(K)\) as an improvement of the previous value \(T(K-1)\), and the function that doubles the number of intervals until two successive approximations are sufficiently close.

def trapezoid_refine(f, a, b, T_old, K):
    """Return T(K), the trapezoidal rule with 2**(K - 1) intervals,
    given T_old = T(K - 1)."""
    n = 2 ** (K - 1)                    # the number of intervals
    h = (b - a) / n                     # the step
    x_new = a + h * np.arange(1, n, 2)  # the new points x_j = a + j h with odd j
    return T_old / 2 + h * np.sum(f(x_new))


def trapezoid_tol(f, a, b, tol=1e-8, min_iterations=5, max_iterations=25):
    """Approximate the integral of f over [a, b] by the composite trapezoidal rule.

    The number of intervals is doubled until the relative difference of two
    successive approximations does not exceed tol, but at least min_iterations
    approximations are computed. If this does not happen after max_iterations
    approximations (2**(max_iterations - 1) intervals), an error is raised.
    """
    T = (b - a) * (f(a) + f(b)) / 2     # T(1)
    for K in range(2, max_iterations + 1):
        T_new = trapezoid_refine(f, a, b, T, K)
        if K >= min_iterations and abs(T_new - T) <= tol * abs(T_new):
            return T_new
        T = T_new

    raise RuntimeError(f"No convergence; the last approximation is {T}.")


for tol in [1e-8, 1e-11]:
    result = trapezoid_tol(np.exp, 0, 1, tol)
    print(f"tol = {tol:.0e}: {result:.15f}, error {abs(result - exact):.1e}")
tol = 1e-08: 1.718281830592745, error 2.1e-09
tol = 1e-11: 1.718281828461129, error 2.1e-12

Two details of the stopping condition deserve a comment. First, I use <= and not <: if the integral is zero (e.g., for an odd function on \([-1,1]\)), all the approximations may be exactly zero, and the condition 0 < tol * 0 would never be satisfied. Second, I do not stop before min_iterations approximations are computed. The reason is that the first approximations use only a few values of \(f\), and two of them can be close by pure chance. For example, for \(f(x)=1+\sin^2 2\pi x\) on \([0,1]\) I have \(T(1)=T(2)=1\), since \(\sin 2\pi x=0\) at \(x=0,\frac 12,1\), whereas the integral is \(1.5\); without the minimal number of iterations the function would stop immediately with the answer \(1\). No stopping rule that uses only finitely many values of \(f\) can be completely reliable (see Exercise 14.6), but this one is reasonable for not too complicated functions.

Can I do better? Actually, there are quite deep theoretical reasons that show how a more efficient code can be written. The truth is that when one approximates an integral with the composite trapezoidal rule, the actual error has the following representation: \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x =T(N)+c_1h^2+c_2h^4+c_3h^6+\ldots; \] note the absence of odd powers of \(h\). (I found the first coefficient in Section 14.5: \(c_1=-(f'(b)-f'(a))/12\).) Similarly, one has \[ \int_a^b f(x)\D x= T(N/2)+c_1(2h)^2+c_2(2h)^4+c_3(2h)^6+\ldots, \] because halving the number of intervals doubles the step. Denoting the exact value of the integral by \(I\), I get \[ I=\frac{4T(N)-T(N/2)}{3}+\tilde{c}_2h^4+\ldots, \] that is, the formula \[ I\approx\frac{4T(N)-T(N/2)}{3} \] has order four (in fact, it is exactly the composite Simpson’s rule \(S(N)\), see Exercise 14.2), and it uses the trapezoidal rule with the number of intervals doubled at each step, exactly as in my code above. Hence I can write a new function that takes advantage of this.

def simpson_tol(f, a, b, tol=1e-8, min_iterations=5, max_iterations=25):
    """Approximate the integral of f over [a, b] by the composite Simpson's rule
    S(N) = (4 T(N) - T(N/2)) / 3, doubling N as in trapezoid_tol."""
    T = (b - a) * (f(a) + f(b)) / 2     # T(1)
    S = T                               # not used before S(2) is computed
    for K in range(2, max_iterations + 1):
        T_new = trapezoid_refine(f, a, b, T, K)
        S_new = (4 * T_new - T) / 3
        if K >= min_iterations and abs(S_new - S) <= tol * abs(S_new):
            return S_new
        T, S = T_new, S_new

    raise RuntimeError(f"No convergence; the last approximation is {S}.")


for tol in [1e-8, 1e-11]:
    result = simpson_tol(np.exp, 0, 1, tol)
    print(f"tol = {tol:.0e}: {result:.15f}, error {abs(result - exact):.1e}")
tol = 1e-08: 1.718281829028015, error 5.7e-10
tol = 1e-11: 1.718281828459184, error 1.4e-13

The function simpson_tol requires far fewer evaluations of \(f\) than trapezoid_tol: for my example, with tol=1e-8 it uses \(65\) values of \(f\) instead of \(8193\), and with tol=1e-11 it uses \(513\) values instead of \(262145\), and its results are more accurate. The same idea can be applied again to the values \(S(N)\), which gives a formula of order 6, and so on; this is called the Romberg method.
