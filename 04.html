In this section I briefly describe a few methods, other than bisection, to find roots of scalar equations. By no means will I try to cover all the possible details; the goal here is the practicality of the methods and their implementations, and not establishing theorems. I will deal with some of the theoretical aspects later.

4.1Newton–Raphson method

I start with another method that is sometimes treated in calculus courses. The geometrical idea of this method can be seen in Fig. 4.1.

In words, I start with an assumption that I bracketed my root between \(a\) and \(b\) (that is, \(f(a)f(b)<0\)). After it I choose any point from the interval \([a,b]\) to be my starting point \(x_0\); in the figure I simply take \(x_0=b\), but any other choice is acceptable. After it, I find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at the point \((x_0,f(x_0))\). Recall that this equation has the form \[ y-f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x-x_0). \] Next, I find the point of intersection of this tangent line with the \(x\)-axis, and this point will be my next approximation of the unknown root; I will call it \(x_1\). Since at this point \(y=0\), I find \[ -f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x_1-x_0), \] or, solving for \(x_1\), \[ x_1=x_0-\frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}\,. \] I can continue this process by finding the tangent line at the point \((x_1,f(x_1))\), then \(x_2\), etc. In general, I have the following recurrent formula for the sequence of my (I hope) approximations of the unknown root \(\hat x\): \begin{equation}\label{eq4:1} x_n=x_{n-1}-\frac{f(x_{n-1})}{f'(x_{n-1})}\,,\quad n=1,2,\ldots \end{equation} Note that in order to apply the Newton–Raphson method, we must assume that the function is not just continuous but also differentiable, since we need to compute its derivative at each step. So, why do we care about this specific method if we already saw that the simple and robust bisection works great on an even larger set of functions? Here is an explanation by an example.

Consider again the equation \[ x^2-2=0, \] and take \(x_0=2\). Since \(f'(x)=2x\), \(\eqref{eq4:1}\) becomes \[ x_{n}=x_{n-1}-\frac{x_{n-1}^2-2}{2x_{n-1}}=\frac 12\left(x_{n-1}+\frac{2}{x_{n-1}}\right). \] You may recognize the Babylonian method from Exercise 2.14. Here is my sequence \((x_n)\):

# Newton-Raphson method for x^2 - 2 = 0, starting from x0 = 2

import math

x = 2.0
print(f"x0 = {x}")

for n in range(1, 5):
    x = (x + 2 / x) / 2     # x_n computed from x_{n-1}
    print(f"x{n} = {x}")

print(f"sqrt(2) = {math.sqrt(2)}")
x0 = 2.0
x1 = 1.5
x2 = 1.4166666666666665
x3 = 1.4142156862745097
x4 = 1.4142135623746899
sqrt(2) = 1.4142135623730951

You can see that already \(x_2\) has two correct digits after the decimal point, \(x_3\) has 5 correct digits, and \(x_4\) has 11 correct digits: the number of correct digits roughly doubles at each step. To get 11 correct digits, bisection needs many more than 4 steps (see Exercise 4.1). This is the main reason why the Newton–Raphson method is often preferred to bisection (or to any other method with linear convergence, see Section 4.5 below).

If this method is so great, why do we even care about anything else? There should be a catch. Actually, there are quite a few things that may go wrong.

First, we need to know the derivative. This is very easy for a function like \(f(x)=x^2-2\), but in other situations it can be quite difficult to figure out. Second, since we are dividing by \(f'(x_n)\), we should assume that \(f'(x_n)\neq 0\) at every step. But the biggest problem is illustrated in Fig. 4.2.

Note that already the second approximation \(x_2\) leaves the bracketing interval \([a,b]\), and from this point on nothing guarantees that the method converges! The outcome critically depends on the given function \(f\), the chosen interval \([a,b]\), and the initial guess \(x_0\).

4.2Secant method

A close relative of the Newton–Raphson method is the secant method, which has the advantage of not requiring the knowledge of the derivative of a function. The idea is given in Fig. 4.3.

In words, I start with two initial points (my usual \(a\) and \(b\)) and decide which one is \(x_0\) and which one is \(x_1\) (there is some freedom of choice here but no perfect recipe). After it I find the equation of the straight line (secant line, hence the name of the method) connecting the points \((x_0,f(x_0))\) and \((x_1,f(x_1))\). This equation has the form \[ y=f(x_0)+\frac{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}{x_1-x_0}(x-x_0). \] Compare this formula with the equation of the tangent line in the Newton–Raphson method: in a sense, here I approximate my derivative by the difference quotient \[ f'(x_0)\approx \frac{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}{x_1-x_0}\,. \] Next I find the intersection of the secant line with the \(x\)-axis, and this will be my next approximation: \[ x_2= x_0-f(x_0)\frac{x_1-x_0}{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}\,. \] In general I have the following recurrent formula: \begin{equation}\label{eq4:2} x_{n+1}=x_{n-1}-f(x_{n-1})\frac{x_{n}-x_{n-1}}{f(x_{n})-f(x_{n-1})}\,,\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots \end{equation} So, we have some advantages; what is the disadvantage? Similarly to the Newton–Raphson method, the secant method does not have to converge. In Fig. 4.3, \(f(x_1)\) and \(f(x_2)\) are both positive, so the interval between \(x_2\) and \(x_1\) does not bracket the root anymore, and \(x_3\) is far outside of \([a,b]\) (see also Exercise 4.2).

4.3False position method

The false position method (also known by its Latin name regula falsi) is almost identical to the secant method, with the only difference that at each step the root is bracketed. That is, the next approximation of the root is computed with the help of \(\eqref{eq4:2}\), but instead of \(x_n\) and \(x_{n-1}\) one uses \(x_n\) and the most recent iterate \(x_k\), \(k<n\), such that \(f(x_n)\) and \(f(x_k)\) have opposite signs. See Fig. 4.4 for the illustration and compare it with Fig. 4.3, where the same function is used.

Since the root is bracketed at every step, the false position method always converges for a continuous function (as the bisection method does), but since at some steps it uses older points than the secant method, it can be somewhat slower. There are also situations when bisection easily beats both the secant and false position methods in terms of the number of steps required to get sufficiently close to the root (see Exercise 4.3).

4.4Other methods

Bisection, Newton–Raphson, secant, and false position methods are the classical methods, which you will find in any textbook on numerical analysis. You should not think, however, that the story ends here. Finding roots of scalar equations is one of the oldest problems of numerical analysis, and yet new methods keep appearing. Let me briefly describe two of them, without any details; you will get a chance to look at them more closely in your projects.

Let me first summarize what we already know. Bisection is slow, but it never fails, and its worst case is known in advance: to reduce the length of the bracketing interval from \(|b-a|\) to \(\epsilon\), it needs about \(\log_2(|b-a|/\epsilon)\) steps. In fact, it is known that no method that uses only the values of \(f\) can guarantee a better worst case than bisection for all continuous functions with \(f(a)f(b)<0\) [4]. The Newton–Raphson and secant methods are much faster when they work, but they may leave the bracketing interval and fail. The false position method keeps the root bracketed, but it can be very slow, because one of the ends of the interval may stay fixed for many steps. So the natural question is: can one have the speed of the secant method together with the reliability of bisection?

Ridders’ method. This method was proposed by C. Ridders in 1979 [3]. At each step it evaluates \(f\) at the ends of the bracketing interval and at its midpoint, and uses these three values to “straighten” the graph of \(f\): the function \(f\) is multiplied by a suitably chosen exponential function, so that the three points of the new graph lie on a straight line. Then, as in the false position method, the intersection of this line with the \(x\)-axis is taken as the next approximation of the root. The resulting point is guaranteed to stay within the bracketing interval, and the new, smaller, bracketing interval is chosen among the available points. As a result, the method keeps the reliability of bisection and false position, does not need the derivative, and converges much faster: one step of Ridders’ method needs two new values of \(f\), but, close to the root, the error is approximately squared at each step. The method is very simple to program, and it is a part of many numerical libraries (e.g., the function ridder in the Python package SciPy).

ITP method. This method was proposed by I. F. D. Oliveira and R. H. C. Takahashi very recently, in 2020 [2], which shows that even the simplest problems of numerical analysis are still an area of active research. The name stands for Interpolate, Truncate, Project, which are the three stages of each step. First, the method computes the false position point (interpolation). Second, it moves this point slightly towards the midpoint of the interval (truncation), which protects the method from the slow convergence of the false position method. Third, it makes sure that the new point is not too far from the midpoint (projection), so that the bracketing interval shrinks at least as fast as the guaranteed worst case of bisection. As a result, the ITP method never needs more steps than bisection (for an appropriate choice of its parameters), and at the same time, for well behaved functions, it converges as fast as the secant method. This is the main advance over the popular earlier hybrid methods, such as Brent’s method [1] (which is, e.g., the function brentq in SciPy): they also combine bisection with interpolation and are fast in practice, but, in the worst case, they can require noticeably more steps than plain bisection.

4.5Order and rate of convergence

I already mentioned several times the speed of convergence, but never defined it precisely. Let me do it here. Assume that my numerical procedure returns a sequence \((x_n)_{n=0}^\infty=x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots\) of approximations of the exact root \(\hat x\) (think about the centers of the intervals for the bisection method, and the sequence of values \(x_n\) for the other methods). At each step I make an absolute error \[ \varepsilon_n = |x_n-\hat x|. \] (Of course, in reality I do not know \(\varepsilon_n\) since I do not know \(\hat x\), but I can often estimate these quantities in practice, and in my test examples I know them precisely.) If my method works, I naturally should have that \(\varepsilon_n\to 0\) as \(n\) grows. But this sequence can approach zero with various speed (whatever it means for sequences); I will give examples later. First the precise definition.

Definition 4.1. Let \((\varepsilon_n)_{n=0}^\infty\) be the sequence of absolute errors for a particular numerical method that converges: \(\varepsilon_n\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\). We say that this method has the order \(q\geq 1\) and the rate of convergence \(\mu\) if \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\left(\varepsilon_{n}\right)^q}=\mu, \] where \(0<\mu<\infty\) (and \(\mu<1\) if \(q=1\)). The method is called linear if \(q=1\) and quadratic if \(q=2\).

Since the bisection method is so simple analytically, I can figure out what this limit should be for it. Since we shrink our intervals by half each time, the absolute errors should also approximately halve at each step, that is, \[ \frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\varepsilon_n}\approx \frac 12, \] therefore, according to the definition above, this method has the order 1 (it is linear) and the rate of convergence \(1/2\).

How can one figure out \(q\) and \(\mu\) in more complicated cases? To answer this question exactly, one usually needs a more involved analysis; examples will be given later. If the method is already implemented, one can try to estimate these quantities. Here is an example of such an estimate for the bisection method.

First, I will need a list of errors. Here is my modified bisection function from Section 3, which performs a given number of bisections and returns the list of errors, given that I already know the exact value of the root.

import math


def f1(x):
    return x * x - 2


def bisection_errors(f, a, b, N, root):
    """Perform N bisections of [a, b] for the function f and return the list
    of the absolute errors |mid - root| of the N midpoints, where root is
    the exact value of the root."""
    if a > b:
        a, b = b, a     # make sure that a is the left end and b is the right one

    fa, fb = f(a), f(b)

    # check that the signs of f(a) and f(b) are opposite
    if (fa < 0) == (fb < 0):
        raise ValueError("The signs of f at the ends of the interval should be opposite.")

    errors = []

    for k in range(N):
        mid = a + (b - a) / 2
        fmid = f(mid)

        errors.append(abs(mid - root))

        if (fa < 0) != (fmid < 0):  # opposite signs of f at a and mid: "move" b
            b = mid
        else:                       # otherwise "move" a
            a, fa = mid, fmid

    return errors


errors = bisection_errors(f1, 1.2, 1.7, 30, math.sqrt(2))
log_errors = [math.log(e) for e in errors]

Here abs is the built-in function that returns the absolute value of a number, and math.log is the natural logarithm. Note that I do not stop if fmid == 0, since I need exactly N errors (for my test function this cannot happen anyway, since \(\sqrt 2\) is not a float number). In the last line I compute the logarithms of the errors. This is important because the errors quickly become very small, and the list errors contains values of dramatically different orders of magnitude, which would be difficult to put in a figure (which I plan to do). Next, I plot \(\log \varepsilon_{n+1}\) versus \(\log \varepsilon_n\).

Why this strange choice? Note that from above I have \[ \varepsilon_{n+1}\approx\frac 12\varepsilon_{n}, \] and after taking logarithms on both sides (here and below \(\log\) denotes the natural logarithm, which is computed in Python by math.log) I find \[ \log\varepsilon_{n+1}\approx \log\frac 12+\log\varepsilon_n. \] That is, if my code works correctly, the points \((\log\varepsilon_n,\log\varepsilon_{n+1})\) should be close to the straight line with the slope 1 and the shift \(\log\frac 12\approx -0.693\).

To make figures, I use the package Matplotlib (more precisely, its part matplotlib.pyplot, which is always imported as plt). I will discuss plotting in more detail later in the course; for now, here is all we need. The command plt.plot(x, y, "bo") plots the points with the coordinates from the lists x and y as blue (b) circles (o), and "r–" would mean a red dashed line. (In the figures in these notes I also set the fonts to match the text; you do not need to do this.)

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.plot(log_errors[:-1], log_errors[1:], "bo")   # all the errors but the last one
                                                  # versus all the errors but the first one
plt.xlabel(r"$\log\varepsilon_n$")
plt.ylabel(r"$\log\varepsilon_{n+1}$")
plt.savefig("04_5.pdf")     # saving the figure in a file to use in these notes
plt.show()

The letter r in front of the strings with the labels tells Python to keep the backslashes as they are, and the text between the dollar signs is typeset as a mathematical formula. The result is in Fig. 4.5.

And indeed I see points along a straight line. Is this the right slope and shift? To answer this question, I use the function polyfit from NumPy, which finds the straight line that fits my points best (this is called linear regression, if you heard these words before).

import numpy as np

slope, shift = np.polyfit(log_errors[:-1], log_errors[1:], 1)   # 1 means a straight line
print(f"slope = {slope}")
print(f"shift = {shift}")

x = [min(log_errors), max(log_errors)]  # two points are enough to draw a straight line
y = [slope * t + shift for t in x]

plt.plot(log_errors[:-1], log_errors[1:], "bo")
plt.plot(x, y, "r--")                   # add my line to the previous figure
plt.xlabel(r"$\log\varepsilon_n$")
plt.ylabel(r"$\log\varepsilon_{n+1}$")
plt.savefig("04_6.pdf")
plt.show()
slope = 0.9883961293784368
shift = -0.7836534441153551

You can see in Fig. 4.6 that the slope is very close to 1, but the shift is not exactly what I expected, even though the fit looks very good. The reason is visible in the figures: the points zigzag around the line. Indeed, the error of the midpoint is bounded by half of the length of the current interval, and this bound is halved exactly at each step; the errors themselves, however, depend on where exactly the root is inside the interval, and the ratio \(\varepsilon_{n+1}/\varepsilon_n\) jumps around \(1/2\) (strictly speaking, the limit in the definition does not exist for the bisection method). So bisection halves the error “on average,” and the shift found by the computer is a reasonable estimate of \(\log\frac 12\).

In general, note that if \[ \frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\left(\varepsilon_{n}\right)^q}\approx \mu \] then \[ \varepsilon_{n+1}\approx \mu \varepsilon_{n}^q, \] and after taking logs \[ \log \varepsilon_{n+1}\approx \log \mu + q\log \varepsilon_{n}. \] That is, the same procedure as above, but for a different method, gives an approximation of \(q\) as the slope of the best fitting straight line, and an approximation of \(\log \mu\) as its shift. You will need to do it in your first project for this semester, which I will discuss next.
