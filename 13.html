13.1Lagrange interpolation polynomial

Consider the following problem: Let \(\bs x=(x_0,\ldots,x_n)\) and \(\bs y=(y_0,\ldots,y_n)\) be two data sets, which represent points on the plane \((x_0,y_0),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)\) (hence \(n+1\) points in total); is it possible to find some polynomial \(p\), which satisfies \[ p(x_i)=y_i,\quad i=0,\ldots,n? \] (i.e., the polynomial passes exactly through these points). Why would I want to do something like this? Naturally, these two data sets can be interpreted as observations from some experiment of an unknown function \(f\). If I am able to construct such a polynomial then I hope I can interpolate the values of my function at other points \(x\neq x_i\) by setting \[ f(x)\approx p(x), \] (of course I need to be careful with how close I am to the actual function \(f\)).

If I am given only one point \((x_0,y_0)\) then it is natural to consider the constant polynomial \(p_0(x)=y_0\) (here and below the index in my polynomial indicates its maximal possible degree) as my best interpolation; if I have two points \((x_0,y_0),\,(x_1,y_1)\) it is natural to consider the straight line connecting these two points (linear interpolation): \[ p_1(x)=y_0 +\frac{y_1-y_0}{x_1-x_0}(x-x_0), \] etc. Therefore it seems plausible to assume that with \(n+1\) points one should be able to construct \(p_n\), i.e., a polynomial of degree at most \(n\), that passes exactly through these points. And indeed this expectation is correct.

Theorem 13.1. Let \(\bs x,\bs y\) be as above with \(x_i\neq x_j\) for \(i\neq j\). Then there exists a unique polynomial \(p_n\) of degree at most \(n\) satisfying \(p_n(x_i)=y_i,\,i=0,\ldots,n\).

Proof. The proof of this theorem is constructive: I will present a formula that actually builds the required polynomial.

Consider \[ L_k(x)=\frac{(x-x_0)\ldots(x-x_{k-1})(x-x_{k+1})\ldots(x-x_n)}{(x_k-x_0)\ldots(x_k-x_{k-1})(x_k-x_{k+1})\ldots(x_k-x_n)}=\prod_{i=0,\,i\neq k}^n \frac{x-x_i}{x_k-x_i}\,,\quad k=0,\ldots,n. \] By construction, \(L_k\) are polynomials of degree \(n\), and they satisfy \[ L_k(x_i)=\begin{cases} 0,\quad&i\neq k,\\ 1,\quad&i=k. \end{cases} \] Now consider \begin{equation}\label{eq13:1} p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^nL_k(x)y_k. \end{equation} Clearly, \(p_n\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\) (as the sum of polynomials of degree \(n\)), and, moreover, \[ p_n(x_k)=y_k,\quad k=0,\ldots,n, \] as required.

What is left to show is that this polynomial is unique. Assume that there is another polynomial \(q_n\) of degree at most \(n\) such that \(q_n(x_i)=y_i\) for all \(i\). Consider their difference \[ p_n(x)-q_n(x)=g_n(x). \] I have that \(g_n(x_i)=y_i-y_i=0\), that is, \(g_n\) has \(n+1\) distinct roots. Since a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(n\) can have at most \(n\) distinct roots, \(g_n\) is identically zero, and hence \(p_n=q_n\), i.e., the required polynomial is unique. ■

Definition 13.2. The polynomial \[ p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^nL_k(x)y_k=\sum_{k=0}^n \left(\prod_{i=0,i\neq k}^n\frac{x-x_i}{x_k-x_i}\right)y_k \] is called the Lagrange interpolation polynomial.

Example 13.3. Let \(f(x)=e^x\) and consider \(\bs x=(-1,0,1)\). My \(\bs y\) data are then \(\bs y=(e^{-1},1,e)\).

According to the formula above, I have \[ L_0(x)=\frac{(x-x_1)(x-x_2)}{(x_0-x_1)(x_0-x_2)}=\frac 12 x(x-1),\quad L_1(x)=1-x^2,\quad L_2(x)=\frac 12 x(x+1). \] Therefore my Lagrange interpolation polynomial is given by \[ p_2(x)=\frac 12x(x-1)e^{-1}+(1-x^2)+\frac 12 x(x+1)e=1+(\sinh 1)x+(\cosh 1-1)x^2, \] see Fig. 13.1.

In the same figure it can be seen that the difference between \(f\) and \(p_2\) is quite noticeable, but the expectation is that if I increase the number of points, the error I am making in replacing \(f\) with its interpolation polynomial will decrease. For this particular example this is true, see Fig. 13.2.

And indeed, for instance, while computing \(p_3(0.5)\) instead of \(f(0.5)\) I make a relative error of only \(0.3\%\), and for \(p_4\) the relative error does not exceed \(0.2\%\) on the whole interval \([-1,1]\), which is quite acceptable for many purposes. Unfortunately, this kind of nice behavior for the interpolation polynomials is not the rule, as the following example shows.

Example 13.4. For this example I will take the classical \[ f(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2} \] on \([a,b]=[-5,5]\). I will be using equally spaced points on \([-5,5]\), i.e., if \(n+1\) is the number of points then \[ x_i=a+i\frac{b-a}{n}\,,\quad i=0,\ldots,n,\quad y_i=f(x_i), \] with \(a=-5\), \(b=5\).

The illustrations for \(n=4,6,8,10,12,14\) are given in Fig. 13.3.

A perfunctory inspection indicates (and the computation in Section 13.2 confirms: this maximum grows from about \(0.44\) for \(n=4\) to about \(7.2\) for \(n=14\)) that although the number of interpolation points increases, the error I am making, namely, \[ \max_{x\in[-5,5]}\left|\frac{1}{1+x^2}-p_n(x)\right|, \] also increases (opposite to our naive expectation). This is known as the Runge phenomenon.

To be able to get estimates of the potential errors, I will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 13.5. Assume that \(f\in C^{n+1}[a,b]\) (this means that \(f\) is an \(n+1\) times continuously differentiable function on the interval \([a,b]\)), let \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\in[a,b]\) be distinct, and let \(p_n\) be the interpolation polynomial of \(f\) at these points. Then \begin{equation}\label{eq13:2} |f(x)-p_n(x)|\leq \frac{M_{n+1}}{(n+1)!}|\pi_{n+1}(x)|,\quad x\in[a,b], \end{equation} where \(\pi_{n+1}(x)=(x-x_0)(x-x_1)\ldots(x-x_n)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n+1\) and \[ M_{n+1}=\max_{x\in[a,b]}\left|f^{(n+1)}(x)\right| \] is the maximum of the \((n+1)\)-st derivative of \(f\) on the given interval \([a,b]\).

Proof. First fix \(x\in [a,b]\). If \(x=x_j\) (i.e., \(x\) is one of the interpolation points) then \(\eqref{eq13:2}\) trivially holds. So assume that \(x\neq x_j\) and hence \(\pi_{n+1}(x)\neq 0\).

Consider \[ F(y)=f(y)-p_n(y)-\lambda \pi_{n+1}(y), \] where I choose \(\lambda\) such that \(F(x)=0\): \[ \lambda=\frac{f(x)-p_n(x)}{\pi_{n+1}(x)}\,. \] Since \(f\in C^{n+1}[a,b]\) and polynomials are infinitely differentiable, also \(F\in C^{n+1}[a,b]\). Moreover, \(F\) is zero at \(n+2\) distinct points (the interpolation points plus \(x\)). By Rolle’s theorem \(F'\) must have at least \(n+1\) distinct zeros in \((a,b)\), \(F''\) must have at least \(n\) distinct zeros, and, continuing, \(F^{(n+1)}\) must have at least one zero \(\xi\in(a,b)\), which depends on \(x\). Since \(p_n\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\), and \(\pi_{n+1}\) is a polynomial of degree \(n+1\) with the leading coefficient 1, I have that \[ p_n^{(n+1)}(y)\equiv 0,\quad \pi_{n+1}^{(n+1)}(y)\equiv(n+1)!, \] and hence \[ 0=F^{(n+1)}(\xi)=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)-\frac{f(x)-p_n(x)}{\pi_{n+1}(x)}(n+1)!, \] or \[ f(x)-p_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\pi_{n+1}(x). \] Taking the absolute values and replacing \(|f^{(n+1)}(\xi)|\) with the maximum \(M_{n+1}\) of the \((n+1)\)-st derivative of \(f\) on \([a,b]\) finishes the proof. ■

Note that if all the interpolation points merge into one point \(x_0\), then \(\pi_{n+1}(x)\) becomes \((x-x_0)^{n+1}\), and the last formula in the proof turns into the Lagrange form of the remainder in Taylor’s formula.

Returning to my example, for the function \(f(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\) one can show that \(|f^{(m)}(x)|\leq m!\) for all \(x\), with equality at \(x=0\) for even \(m\). Hence \(M_{n+1}/(n+1)!\) is close to 1, and the right-hand side of \(\eqref{eq13:2}\) is essentially \(\max_x|\pi_{n+1}(x)|\), which for equally spaced points on \([-5,5]\) grows very fast with \(n\) (e.g., it is about \(3.6\cdot 10^4\) for \(n=8\) and about \(5.9\cdot 10^7\) for \(n=14\)). Therefore, estimate \(\eqref{eq13:2}\) does not guarantee any convergence in this example. Note that a growing upper bound by itself does not prove that the error grows, but the figures show that in this example it does.

Can I somehow mitigate this phenomenon? The answer is surprisingly “yes,” and I will show only the final result, since the actual mathematical explanation of how it all “works” is beyond these lecture notes.

Let me take different interpolation points instead of equally spaced \(x_0,\ldots,x_n,\) which I used before. Namely, I choose the so-called Chebyshev points \begin{equation}\label{eq13:3} x_j = \frac{b-a}{2}\cos\frac{(j+0.5)\pi}{n+1}+\frac{b+a}{2}\,,\quad j=0,\ldots,n. \end{equation}

For this choice my interpolation polynomial behaves much better, see Fig. 13.4: the maximal error decreases from about \(0.40\) for \(n=4\) to about \(0.015\) for \(n=20\) (see Section 13.2). Note that the Chebyshev points are more densely packed near the ends of the interval, exactly where the polynomials with equally spaced points misbehave. In Exercise 13.2 you will see that for the Chebyshev points \(\max_x|\pi_{n+1}(x)|\) is much smaller than for equally spaced points; in fact, it can be proved that the Chebyshev points make this maximum as small as possible.

13.2Python code

All the figures in this section were made with a direct implementation of \(\eqref{eq13:1}\). Here I would like to note that, when dealing with the Lagrange interpolation polynomial, it is better to evaluate \(p_n(x)\) at a given point \(x\) directly by \(\eqref{eq13:1}\) than to first compute the coefficients of the polynomial and then use them to calculate \(p_n(x)\); the reason will be explained in Section 13.4.

Since we now have NumPy (Section 9), I write the function so that x can be either a single number or an array of points.

import numpy as np


def lagrange(x, x_data, y_data):
    """Evaluate the Lagrange interpolation polynomial at x (a number or an array).

    x_data and y_data are arrays of the n + 1 interpolation points and the
    corresponding values; the points must be distinct.
    """
    n = len(x_data) - 1
    p = 0.0
    for k in range(n + 1):
        L = 1.0                     # this becomes L_k(x)
        for i in range(n + 1):
            if i != k:
                L *= (x - x_data[i]) / (x_data[k] - x_data[i])
        p += L * y_data[k]
    return p

Compare the code with formula \(\eqref{eq13:1}\): the outer loop is the sum over \(k\), the inner loop is the product over \(i\neq k\), and the letters are the same as in the formula. The loops run over the \(n+1\) interpolation points, but all the arithmetic with x is elementwise (Section 9). Hence if x is an array of \(1000\) points, then after the first multiplication L becomes an array, and each line of the function processes all \(1000\) points at once. This is a general principle: a function that uses only arithmetic operations and NumPy functions (such as np.exp, but not math.exp, which accepts only numbers) automatically works with arrays. Let me check how much this gains, compared with calling the same function for one point at a time.

import time


def runge(x):
    return 1 / (1 + x**2)


x_data = np.linspace(-5, 5, 15)     # 15 equally spaced points, n = 14
y_data = runge(x_data)
x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 1000)

t0 = time.perf_counter()
y1 = np.array([lagrange(t, x_data, y_data) for t in x])     # one point at a time
t1 = time.perf_counter()
y2 = lagrange(x, x_data, y_data)                            # all the points at once
t2 = time.perf_counter()

print(f"one point at a time: {t1 - t0:.4f} seconds")
print(f"all points at once:  {t2 - t1:.4f} seconds")
print(f"the largest difference: {np.max(np.abs(y1 - y2))}")
one point at a time: 0.0499 seconds
all points at once:  0.0005 seconds
the largest difference: 0.0

The results are identical (here np.max returns the largest element of an array, so the last line computes the largest difference between the elements of the two arrays), and the vectorized call is about two orders of magnitude faster.

Here is how the left part of Fig. 13.2 is made (the figures in these notes are made by the same commands with some additional styling).

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Finally, let me compute the maximal errors for the Runge function, for equally spaced and for the Chebyshev points. The function np.arange(n + 1) returns the array \((0,1,\ldots,n)\) (it is the NumPy analogue of range), and hence the Chebyshev points \(\eqref{eq13:3}\) are computed for all \(j\) in one line, which is just the formula itself. The maximum over \([-5,5]\) is replaced with the maximum over \(2001\) equally spaced points.

def chebyshev_points(a, b, n):
    """Return the n + 1 Chebyshev points (13.3) on [a, b]."""
    j = np.arange(n + 1)
    return (b - a) / 2 * np.cos((j + 0.5) * np.pi / (n + 1)) + (a + b) / 2


a, b = -5, 5
x = np.linspace(a, b, 2001)
y = runge(x)

print("  n   equally spaced   Chebyshev")
for n in [4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 20]:
    x_equal = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)
    x_cheb = chebyshev_points(a, b, n)
    error_equal = np.max(np.abs(y - lagrange(x, x_equal, runge(x_equal))))
    error_cheb = np.max(np.abs(y - lagrange(x, x_cheb, runge(x_cheb))))
    print(f"{n:3d}   {error_equal:14.2e}   {error_cheb:9.2e}")
  n   equally spaced   Chebyshev
  4         4.38e-01    4.02e-01
  6         6.17e-01    2.64e-01
  8         1.05e+00    1.71e-01
 10         1.92e+00    1.09e-01
 12         3.66e+00    6.92e-02

With equally spaced points the error grows with \(n\); with the Chebyshev points it decreases.

13.3Computing the derivatives

The Lagrange interpolation polynomial can be used to compute the derivative of a function \(f\) that is given in a table form. From \(\eqref{eq13:1}\) one can immediately see that \[ p'_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n L_k'(x)y_k, \] hence all I need is to calculate \[ L'_k(x)=\sum_{i=0,i\neq k}^n\frac{1}{x_k-x_i}\prod_{m=0,m\neq i,m\neq k}^n\frac{x-x_m}{x_k-x_m}\,. \] Under suitable conditions on \(f\) and on the interpolation points, \(p'_n(x)\) approximates \(f'(x)\) well, but, as the Runge phenomenon shows, this is not true in general. Moreover, without going into any technical details, I would like to mention that numerical differentiation is a numerically unstable procedure (recall the numerical differentiation example in Section 10), and should always be performed with caution, since it is usually true that any measurement errors as well as inevitable roundoff errors due to floating-point arithmetic will be magnified during numerical differentiation.

13.4Coefficients of the interpolation polynomial

I mentioned that one rarely needs the explicit expressions for the coefficients of the interpolation polynomial. The value \(p_n(x)\) is usually computed directly, without the knowledge of the explicit form of \(p_n\). Assume for a second that for some reason I need these coefficients. How to find them? Probably the most natural way is to start with the polynomial \[ p_n(x)=a_n x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{1}x+a_0 \] and realize that the data I have allow one to write my problem as a system of \(n+1\) linear equations with \(n+1\) unknowns \((a_0,\ldots,a_n)\), in the matrix form \[ \begin{bmatrix} x_0^n & x_0^{n-1} & \ldots & x_0 & 1 \\ x_1^n & x_1^{n-1} & \ldots & x_1 & 1 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \\ x_{n-1}^n & x_{n-1}^{n-1} & \ldots & x_{n-1} & 1 \\ x_n^n & x_{n}^{n-1} & \ldots & x_n & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}\cdot \begin{bmatrix} a_n \\ a_{n-1} \\ \vdots \\ a_1 \\ a_0 \\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} y_0 \\ y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_{n-1} \\ y_{n} \\ \end{bmatrix}. \] The matrix of this system has a special form and is called a Vandermonde matrix. By Theorem 13.1 this system has a unique solution if the points \(x_i\) are distinct. When solving it, however, one has to remember that the Vandermonde matrix can be ill-conditioned (in the sense of Section 11) even for moderate values of \(n\), so care should be exercised.

NumPy builds this matrix with the function np.vander, with the columns in exactly the order above, and the function np.polyval(coefficients, x) evaluates a polynomial with the coefficients \(a_n,\ldots,a_0\) (in this order, the same as for np.polyfit in Section 4) at a number or an array x. Let me compute the condition numbers \(\kappa_\infty\) (recall Section 11) of the Vandermonde matrices for the Runge function with equally spaced points on \([-5,5]\), and compare the values of the polynomial computed from the coefficients with the values computed by lagrange (I continue with the functions and the array x from Section 13.2).

print("  n   condition number   difference")
for n in [5, 10, 15, 20]:
    x_data = np.linspace(-5, 5, n + 1)
    V = np.vander(x_data)                   # the Vandermonde matrix
    coefficients = np.linalg.solve(V, runge(x_data))
    p_from_coefficients = np.polyval(coefficients, x)
    p_from_lagrange = lagrange(x, x_data, runge(x_data))
    difference = np.max(np.abs(p_from_coefficients - p_from_lagrange))
    print(f"{n:3d}   {np.linalg.cond(V, np.inf):16.1e}   {difference:10.1e}")
  n   condition number   difference
  5            5.4e+03      5.4e-16
 10            4.2e+07      9.0e-14
 15            4.1e+11      1.2e-12
 20            4.4e+15      2.9e-10

The condition number grows exponentially with \(n\), as for the Hilbert matrices in Section 11 (although somewhat slower), and for \(n=20\) the rule of thumb from Section 11 does not guarantee a single correct digit in the computed coefficients. Surprisingly, the values of the polynomial computed from these coefficients still agree with the values computed by lagrange to about \(10^{-10}\): the errors in the coefficients largely cancel when the polynomial is evaluated at the points of the interval (recall from Section 11 that the estimate gives the worst case). This is not something to rely on, and it is one more reason to evaluate \(p_n(x)\) directly, without computing its coefficients. (By the way, np.polyfit(x_data, y_data, n) with \(n+1\) points also returns the coefficients of the interpolation polynomial, see Exercise 13.5.)

Exercise 13.4 (The barycentric formula). Let \(w_k=1/\prod_{i\neq k}(x_k-x_i)\), \(k=0,\ldots,n\). Show that for \(x\neq x_k\) \[ p_n(x)=\left.\sum_{k=0}^n \frac{w_ky_k}{x-x_k}\right/\sum_{k=0}^n \frac{w_k}{x-x_k}\,. \] (Hint: write \(L_k(x)=\pi_{n+1}(x)w_k/(x-x_k)\) and apply \(\eqref{eq13:1}\) also to the constant function \(f(x)=1\), whose interpolation polynomial is \(1\).) Implement this formula: compute the numbers \(w_k\) once, and then evaluate \(p_n\) for a whole array x that does not contain the interpolation points. How many operations per point does it need, compared with lagrange? Compare the results and the speed of the two functions.
