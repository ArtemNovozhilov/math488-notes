12.1Motivation

In Sections 7–9 I solved systems \(\bs{Ax}=\bs b\) with a general \(n\times n\) matrix \(\bs A\), and in Section 8 I showed that Gaussian elimination (equivalently, the \(\bs{LU}\) factorization) requires approximately \(2n^3/3\) elementary operations. In many applications, however, the matrix \(\bs A\) has a very special structure, and most of its elements are zero. The simplest and one of the most important examples is a tridiagonal matrix, whose nonzero elements can only be on the main diagonal and on the two diagonals next to it: \begin{equation}\label{eq12:1} \bs A=\begin{bmatrix} d_1 & c_1 & & & \\ a_2 & d_2 & c_2 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & a_{n-1} & d_{n-1} & c_{n-1}\\ & & & a_n & d_n \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation} i.e., \(a_{ij}=0\) if \(|i-j|>1\) (the empty places are zeros). I denote the elements below the main diagonal by \(a_i\), the diagonal elements by \(d_i\), and the elements above the main diagonal by \(c_i\), so that row \(i\) of \(\bs A\) contains \(a_i,d_i,c_i\); it is convenient to set \(a_1=c_n=0\). Such matrices appear, e.g., when one approximates the derivatives of an unknown function by finite differences (recall Section 10), and I will need them in Section 16 to construct the cubic splines.

A tridiagonal matrix is described by about \(3n\) numbers instead of \(n^2\), and the question is whether this can be used to solve the system faster. The answer is a resounding yes: the number of operations drops from \(2n^3/3\) to about \(8n\).

12.2The \(\bs{LU}\) factorization of a tridiagonal matrix

Recall the formulas of the \(\bs{LU}\) factorization from Section 9, where \(\bs L\) is unit lower triangular and \(\bs U\) is upper triangular: \begin{equation}\label{eq12:2} u_{ij}=a_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}l_{ik}u_{kj},\quad i\leq j,\qquad l_{ij}=\frac{1}{u_{jj}}\left(a_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}l_{ik}u_{kj}\right),\quad i>j \end{equation} (in Section 9 the expression in the parentheses was denoted \(v_{ij}\); recall also that \(l_{ij}\) are exactly the multipliers of Gaussian elimination from Section 7). Let me see what these formulas give for the matrix \(\eqref{eq12:1}\).

Proposition 12.1. Let \(\bs A\) be the tridiagonal matrix \(\eqref{eq12:1}\), and assume that its \(\bs{LU}\) factorization exists (i.e., formulas \(\eqref{eq12:2}\) never require a division by zero). Then \[ \bs L=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & \\ l_2 & 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & l_n & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad \bs U=\begin{bmatrix} u_1 & c_1 & & \\ & u_2 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & c_{n-1}\\ & & & u_n \end{bmatrix}, \] where \begin{equation}\label{eq12:3} u_1=d_1,\qquad l_i=\frac{a_i}{u_{i-1}}\,,\quad u_i=d_i-l_ic_{i-1},\quad i=2,\ldots,n. \end{equation} That is, \(\bs L\) has nonzero elements only on the main diagonal and right below it, \(\bs U\) only on the main diagonal and right above it, and the elements above the main diagonal of \(\bs U\) are the same as in \(\bs A\).

Proof. I use induction over the rows, computing in each row \(i\) first the elements \(l_{ij}\), \(j<i\), and then \(u_{ij}\), \(j\geq i\) (formulas \(\eqref{eq12:2}\) use only the elements computed before). Row 1 of \(\bs U\) is row 1 of \(\bs A\) (the sum in \(\eqref{eq12:2}\) is empty), i.e., \(u_{11}=d_1\), \(u_{12}=c_1\), and \(u_{1j}=0\) for \(j>2\). Assume that the statement is proved for the rows \(1,\ldots,i-1\), and consider row \(i\). By the induction assumption, \(l_{ik}\) with \(k<i-1\) are computed by the same formulas as below, so let me go from left to right. For \(j<i-1\) the sum in the formula for \(l_{ij}\) contains only \(l_{ik}\) with \(k<j\), which are already shown to be zero, and \(a_{ij}=0\); hence \(l_{ij}=0\). For \(j=i-1\) the sum again contains only zero terms, hence \(l_{i,i-1}=a_i/u_{i-1,i-1}\). In the formula for \(u_{ij}\), \(j\geq i\), the only term of the sum that can be nonzero is \(l_{i,i-1}u_{i-1,j}\), and by the induction assumption \(u_{i-1,i}=c_{i-1}\) and \(u_{i-1,j}=0\) for \(j>i\). Therefore \(u_{ii}=d_i-l_ic_{i-1}\), \(u_{i,i+1}=c_i\), and \(u_{ij}=a_{ij}=0\) for \(j>i+1\). ■

Exactly the same can be seen from Gaussian elimination: at step \(i\) only one element below the pivot \(u_i\) is nonzero, namely, \(a_{i+1}\), and subtracting \(l_{i+1}\) times row \(i\) from row \(i+1\) changes only one element, \(d_{i+1}\), since row \(i\) contains only two nonzero elements, \(u_i\) and \(c_i\). In the code gauss_numpy from Section 9 this means that the loops over j and k each do only one useful step, and all the other steps subtract zeros from zeros.

Now the system \(\bs{Ax}=\bs b\) is solved as in Section 9: first \(\bs{Ly}=\bs b\) by forward substitution, then \(\bs{Ux}=\bs y\) by back substitution. Since every row of \(\bs L\) and \(\bs U\) has at most two nonzero elements, both substitutions are very simple: \begin{equation}\label{eq12:4} y_1=b_1,\quad y_i=b_i-l_iy_{i-1},\quad i=2,\ldots,n, \end{equation} \begin{equation}\label{eq12:5} x_n=\frac{y_n}{u_n}\,,\quad x_i=\frac{y_i-c_ix_{i+1}}{u_i}\,,\quad i=n-1,\ldots,1. \end{equation} Formulas \(\eqref{eq12:3}\)–\(\eqref{eq12:5}\) are often called the Thomas algorithm (or the tridiagonal matrix algorithm). Note that the forward substitution \(\eqref{eq12:4}\) can be performed together with the factorization \(\eqref{eq12:3}\), in the same loop, and that the matrix \(\bs L\) is not even stored: I need each \(l_i\) only once.

Example 12.2. Let me solve \[ \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & 0\\ 1 & 4 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\bs x=\begin{bmatrix}5\\6\\5\end{bmatrix}, \] whose exact solution is \(\bs x=(1,1,1)^\top\). I have \(u_1=4\), \(y_1=5\); next, \(l_2=1/4\), \(u_2=4-\frac14=\frac{15}{4}\), \(y_2=6-\frac54=\frac{19}{4}\); next, \(l_3=\frac{4}{15}\), \(u_3=4-\frac{4}{15}=\frac{56}{15}\), \(y_3=5-\frac{4}{15}\cdot\frac{19}{4}=\frac{56}{15}\). Back substitution gives \(x_3=1\), \(x_2=\left(\frac{19}{4}-1\right)\big/\frac{15}{4}=1\), \(x_1=(5-1)/4=1\).

12.3The number of operations

Let me count the elementary operations as in Section 8, multiplications/divisions and additions/subtractions separately. For each \(i=2,\ldots,n\), formulas \(\eqref{eq12:3}\) require one division, one multiplication, and one subtraction, and \(\eqref{eq12:4}\) one multiplication and one subtraction. In \(\eqref{eq12:5}\) I need one division for \(x_n\), and one multiplication, one subtraction, and one division for each of \(x_{n-1},\ldots,x_1\). In total, \[ M=2(n-1)+(n-1)+1+2(n-1)=5n-4,\quad A=(n-1)+(n-1)+(n-1)=3n-3, \] and \[ T_{\textup{tridiagonal}}=M+A=8n-7. \] The number of operations is proportional to \(n\), not to \(n^3\). For instance, for \(n=1000\) I need \(7993\) operations, whereas Gaussian elimination for a full matrix needs \(T_{\textup{Gauss}}(1000)=668165500\) (Section 8), i.e., about \(80000\) times more. Note that the general algorithm does not “know” that the matrix is tridiagonal, and spends almost all of its time on subtracting zeros from zeros.

The memory requirements are equally different: I need to store four arrays of length \(n\) (\(a_i\), \(d_i\), \(c_i\), \(b_i\); the arrays \(u_i\), \(y_i\), \(x_i\) can even overwrite them), instead of \(n^2\) numbers. For \(n=10^6\) a full matrix of float numbers (8 bytes each) would occupy \(8\cdot 10^{12}\) bytes, i.e., 8 terabytes, while the four arrays occupy only \(32\) megabytes.

12.4Diagonal dominance: no pivoting is necessary

In Section 9 I showed that the \(\bs{LU}\) factorization without pivoting can fail (if some \(u_i=0\)) or give inaccurate results (if some \(u_i\) is very small, so that large numbers appear in the computation, recall Section 10), and that in general one has to use partial pivoting. For a tridiagonal matrix pivoting is unpleasant: if I switch rows \(i\) and \(i+1\), the new row \(i\) contains a nonzero element \(c_{i+1}\) in column \(i+2\), and \(\bs U\) gets a second nonzero diagonal above the main one, i.e., the simple structure above is destroyed. Fortunately, for a very important class of matrices pivoting is not necessary.

Definition 12.3. A matrix \(\bs A\) is called (strictly, by rows) diagonally dominant if in each row the absolute value of the diagonal element is larger than the sum of the absolute values of all the other elements: \[ |a_{ii}|>\sum_{j\neq i}|a_{ij}|,\quad i=1,\ldots,n. \] For the tridiagonal matrix \(\eqref{eq12:1}\) this means that \(|d_i|>|a_i|+|c_i|\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\) (recall that \(a_1=c_n=0\)).

For instance, the matrix in Example 12.2 is diagonally dominant, since \(4>1+1\).

Theorem 12.4. Let the tridiagonal matrix \(\eqref{eq12:1}\) be diagonally dominant. Then the numbers \(u_i\) in \(\eqref{eq12:3}\) satisfy \[ |c_i|<|u_i|\leq|d_i|+|a_i|,\quad i=1,\ldots,n. \] In particular, all \(u_i\neq 0\), the Thomas algorithm \(\eqref{eq12:3}\)–\(\eqref{eq12:5}\) can always be performed without pivoting, and \(\bs A\) is invertible.

Proof. I use induction. For \(i=1\) I have \(u_1=d_1\) and \(|c_1|<|d_1|=|d_1|+|a_1|\). Assume that \(|u_{i-1}|>|c_{i-1}|\). Then \[ |u_i|=\left|d_i-\frac{a_ic_{i-1}}{u_{i-1}}\right|\geq|d_i|-|a_i|\frac{|c_{i-1}|}{|u_{i-1}|}\geq |d_i|-|a_i|>|c_i|, \] since \(|c_{i-1}|/|u_{i-1}|<1\) and the matrix is diagonally dominant. Similarly, \[ |u_i|\leq|d_i|+|a_i|\frac{|c_{i-1}|}{|u_{i-1}|}\leq|d_i|+|a_i|. \] Finally, \(\det\bs A=\det\bs L\det\bs U=u_1u_2\ldots u_n\neq 0\). ■

The theorem says more than “no division by zero”. The pivots \(u_i\) are not small (at least \(|c_i|\), and, as the proof shows, at least \(|d_i|-|a_i|\)), and none of the elements of \(\bs U\) is larger than \(|d_i|+|a_i|\), i.e., no large numbers appear during the computation. Exactly such large numbers were the source of the trouble without pivoting in Sections 9 and 10, and one can show that for diagonally dominant matrices the algorithm is stable, i.e., its rounding errors are not amplified. Without diagonal dominance the algorithm may fail even for a nonsingular matrix: for instance, for the tridiagonal matrix with \(d_1=0\), \(c_1=a_2=1\) I get \(u_1=0\) immediately.

As I will show in Section 16, the system for the natural cubic splines has a tridiagonal matrix with the elements \(2(h_{i-1}+h_i)\) on the main diagonal and \(h_{i-1}\), \(h_i\) next to it, where \(h_i>0\) are the distances between the interpolation points; such a matrix is clearly diagonally dominant. The matrix in Example 12.2 is exactly this matrix for equally spaced points (divided by \(h\)).

12.5Python code

I store the tridiagonal matrix as three arrays a, d, c of length \(n\), so that row i of the matrix contains a[i], d[i], c[i] (as usual in Python, the indexes start with 0, so that a[i] is \(a_{i+1}\) etc.); the elements a[0] and c[n - 1] are not used. The multiplier \(l_i\) is called m, as in Section 7 (PEP 8 recommends not to use the letter l as a name, since it is easily confused with 1).

import time
import numpy as np


def tridiagonal_solve(a: np.ndarray, d: np.ndarray, c: np.ndarray,
                      b: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Solve the system Ax = b with a tridiagonal matrix A by the Thomas algorithm.

    Row i of A contains a[i], d[i], c[i] (below, on, and above the main diagonal);
    a[0] and c[n - 1] are not used. No pivoting is done, hence the matrix should
    be, e.g., diagonally dominant.
    """
    n = len(d)
    u = np.zeros(n)     # the main diagonal of U
    y = np.zeros(n)     # the solution of Ly = b
    x = np.zeros(n)     # the solution of Ux = y

    # the LU factorization and the forward substitution, (12.3) and (12.4)
    u[0] = d[0]
    y[0] = b[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        if u[i - 1] == 0:
            raise ZeroDivisionError(f"Zero on the main diagonal of U in row {i - 1}.")
        m = a[i] / u[i - 1]             # the multiplier l_i
        u[i] = d[i] - m * c[i - 1]
        y[i] = b[i] - m * y[i - 1]

    # the back substitution (12.5)
    x[n - 1] = y[n - 1] / u[n - 1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (y[i] - c[i] * x[i + 1]) / u[i]
    return x


a = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 1.0])       # the system from Example 12.2
d = np.array([4.0, 4.0, 4.0])
c = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 0.0])
b = np.array([5.0, 6.0, 5.0])
print(tridiagonal_solve(a, d, c, b))
[1. 1. 1.]

Note that the loops here cannot be vectorized (compare with Section 9): each step uses the result of the previous one. This is not a problem, since there are only about \(8n\) operations anyway.

To test the function on larger systems, I take random diagonally dominant matrices: the elements \(a_i\) and \(c_i\) are random numbers from \([0,1)\), and \(d_i=a_i+c_i+1\). As in Section 7, I choose the exact solution \(\bs{\hat x}=(1,\ldots,1)^\top\); then \(b_i=a_i+d_i+c_i\) (this is why it is convenient to set a[0] and c[n - 1] to zero). First I compare my function with np.linalg.solve, for which I need the full matrix; it is built by the function np.diag(v, k), which creates a matrix with the elements of v on the diagonal number k (k = 0 is the main diagonal, k = 1 is the one above it, and k = -1 is the one below it).

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)


def random_tridiagonal_system(n):
    """Return a, d, c, b of a random diagonally dominant system.

    The exact solution of the system is (1, ..., 1).
    """
    a = rng.random(n)
    c = rng.random(n)
    a[0] = 0
    c[n - 1] = 0
    d = a + c + 1
    b = a + d + c
    return a, d, c, b


n = 1000
a, d, c, b = random_tridiagonal_system(n)
A = np.diag(a[1:], -1) + np.diag(d) + np.diag(c[:-1], 1)    # the full matrix

t0 = time.perf_counter()
x1 = tridiagonal_solve(a, d, c, b)
t1 = time.perf_counter()
x2 = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
t2 = time.perf_counter()

print(f"tridiagonal_solve: error {np.max(np.abs(x1 - 1)):.1e}, {t1 - t0:.4f} seconds")
print(f"np.linalg.solve:   error {np.max(np.abs(x2 - 1)):.1e}, {t2 - t1:.4f} seconds")
tridiagonal_solve: error 5.6e-16, 0.0015 seconds
np.linalg.solve:   error 5.6e-16, 0.0314 seconds

Both solutions are accurate to the last digits (the condition number of these matrices is small, recall Section 11), and my simple function with Python loops is faster than the professional, compiled np.linalg.solve applied to the full matrix: a good algorithm beats a good implementation of a worse algorithm. On the other hand, about \(8000\) operations in about a millisecond is only several million operations per second, which shows again how slow Python loops are (Section 9). In practice one uses a compiled implementation, e.g., the function solve_banded from the package SciPy (Exercise 12.6).

Finally, let me check that the time grows linearly with \(n\). For \(n=10^6\) the full matrix could not even be stored.

for n in [10**4, 10**5, 10**6]:
    a, d, c, b = random_tridiagonal_system(n)
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    x = tridiagonal_solve(a, d, c, b)
    t1 = time.perf_counter()
    print(f"n = {n:7d}: error {np.max(np.abs(x - 1)):.1e}, {t1 - t0:.3f} seconds")
n =   10000: error 4.4e-16, 0.014 seconds
n =  100000: error 6.7e-16, 0.173 seconds
n = 1000000: error 6.7e-16, 1.565 seconds

When \(n\) is multiplied by \(10\), the time is multiplied by about \(10\), as predicted.

To see why diagonal dominance (or pivoting) matters, consider the system \[ \begin{bmatrix}10^{-20} & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\bs x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\ 2\end{bmatrix}, \] whose solution is very close to \((1,1)^\top\), and whose matrix is not diagonally dominant (its first row).

a = np.array([0.0, 1.0])
d = np.array([1e-20, 1.0])
c = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
b = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
print(tridiagonal_solve(a, d, c, b))
[0. 1.]

Here \(u_1=10^{-20}\) is tiny, hence \(l_2=10^{20}\) and \(u_2=1-10^{20}\) are huge, the number \(1\) is lost in the subtraction (recall Section 10), and the first component of the solution is completely wrong. The matrix is perfectly well-conditioned (\(\kappa_\infty\approx 4\)), and with partial pivoting (switching the two rows) the problem disappears.

12.6Exercises

Exercise 12.1. Show that the matrix \[ \bs A_n=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & \ddots & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & -1\\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \] of size \(n\times n\) is not diagonally dominant, but nevertheless the Thomas algorithm can be performed: prove that \(u_i=(i+1)/i\). Find \(\det\bs A_n\). Check your answers numerically.

Exercise 12.2. Show that the numbers \(u_i\) in \(\eqref{eq12:3}\) are the same for the matrices \(\bs A\) and \(\bs A^\top\). Conclude that Theorem 12.4 also holds for matrices that are diagonally dominant by columns, i.e., \(|d_i|>|c_{i-1}|+|a_{i+1}|\) (with \(c_0=a_{n+1}=0\)).

Exercise 12.3. Find a nonsingular \(3\times 3\) tridiagonal matrix with all \(d_i\neq 0\) for which the function tridiagonal_solve stops with the error ZeroDivisionError. What does partial pivoting do with this matrix?

Exercise 12.4. In applications, one often has to solve many systems with the same matrix and different right-hand sides. Write two functions: tridiagonal_lu(a, d, c), which returns the arrays of the multipliers \(l_i\) and of the diagonal elements \(u_i\), and tridiagonal_lu_solve(l, u, c, b), which solves the system using them. Count the number of operations of each function.

Exercise 12.5. Measure the time of tridiagonal_solve for several values of \(n\), and find the slope of the line that fits the points \((\log n,\log t)\), as in Section 8. Which slope do you expect?

Exercise 12.6. The function scipy.linalg.solve_banded((1, 1), ab, b) from the package SciPy solves systems with tridiagonal (and, more generally, banded) matrices with a compiled code. Here ab is a \(3\times n\) array whose rows contain the three diagonals: with my notation, ab[0, 1:] = c[:-1], ab[1] = d, and ab[2, :-1] = a[1:]. Compare its speed and accuracy with tridiagonal_solve for \(n=10^6\).
