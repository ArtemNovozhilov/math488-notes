3.1Introductory words on root finding

Now it is finally the time to start looking into some mathematical problems. In this course I will start with a discussion of various approaches to find roots of (transcendental) equations of the form \begin{equation}\label{eq3:1} f(x)=0,\quad x\in X\subseteq \R, \end{equation} where \(f\) will be generally a real-valued function. I write “transcendental” to emphasize that polynomial equations \[ a_n x^n+ a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+ a_1 x+ a_0 = 0, \quad a_j\in \R, \] have their own deeply developed methods to approximate the roots. This is why, for instance, if you ask Python to solve \(x^6+3x^2-x+10=0\) using the function roots from the package NumPy (we will discuss NumPy later; here the polynomial is given by the list of its coefficients, starting with the highest power), it immediately returns all 6 roots (Python writes the imaginary unit as j):

>>> import numpy as np
>>> np.roots([1, 0, 0, 0, 3, -1, 10])     # x^6 + 3x^2 - x + 10
array([-1.30081558+0.91087287j, -1.30081558-0.91087287j,
        1.25795032+0.862663j  ,  1.25795032-0.862663j  ,
        0.04286526+1.30480679j,  0.04286526-1.30480679j])

On the other hand, an attempt to solve \(\tan x=x\), even with the package SymPy, which is designed for symbolic (exact) computations, results in the following:

>>> import sympy
>>> x = sympy.symbols("x")
>>> sympy.solve(sympy.tan(x) - x, x)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
NotImplementedError                       Traceback (most recent call last)
...
NotImplementedError: multiple generators [x, tan(x)]
No algorithms are implemented to solve equation -x + tan(x)

(Here and below ... means that I omitted a few lines of a long error message.) Professional mathematical software, such as Mathematica or Maple, reacts similarly. Hence the first warning: there exists no universal algorithm to find roots of \(\eqref{eq3:1}\) in general, and usually a computer should be assisted by human experience, guesses, and, sometimes, luck, to achieve the desired goal.

For the following I will need a few mathematical facts. First, a root of \(\eqref{eq3:1}\) is a (real for us) constant \(\hat x\in\R\) that turns equation \(\eqref{eq3:1}\) into an identity. Note that in many problems we have expressions of the form “left hand side is equal to the right hand side,” as in, e.g., \(\tan x= - 2 x\), but we can always turn such equations into \(\eqref{eq3:1}\) by moving all the terms to one side. Geometrically, to solve problem \(\eqref{eq3:1}\) means to find the points of intersection of the graph of \(f\) with the \(x\)-axis, whereas solving \(F(x)=G(x)\) geometrically means finding the points of intersection of the graphs of \(F\) and \(G\).

We call a function \(f\) continuous at a point \(x_0\) if for any \(\epsilon>0\) there is a constant \(\delta>0\) such that for all \(x\) satisfying \(|x-x_0|<\delta\) their images satisfy \(|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\epsilon\). The function is continuous on \((a,b)\) if it is continuous at each point of this interval. The function is continuous on \([a,b]\) if it is continuous everywhere inside the interval, continuous from the right at \(a\) (this means that in the definition above we take \(x\) only on the right side of the point \(a\)), and continuous from the left at \(b\). This is a technical (and not easy) mathematical definition; hence it is also important to have some intuitive understanding of continuous functions. For us, a continuous function on \([a,b]\) will be a function whose graph can be drawn without taking your pencil (or pen) from the paper and does not escape to either minus or plus infinity. Most of our familiar functions are continuous in their natural domains; an example of a discontinuous function is, e.g., \(f(x)=1\) if \(x\geq 0\) and \(f(x)=-1\) if \(x<0\) (draw its graph).

The following basic theorem will be important for us (recall that a theorem in mathematics is an important true statement).

Theorem 3.1 (Bolzano). Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\), and assume that \(f(a)f(b)<0\). Then there is a point \(\hat{x}\in(a,b)\) such that \[ f(\hat x)=0. \]

Idea of a proof. Let me assume that \(f(a)<0\); a very similar argument applies to the case \(f(a)>0\). This implies that \(f(b)>0\). Now we can simply find the middle of the interval \((a,b)\): \[ m=\frac{a+b}{2}\,, \] and three cases are possible. First, if we are lucky, \(f(m)=0\), and hence we just found the root: \(\hat x=m\) and we are done. Second, we may have \(f(m)<0\) or, third, \(f(m)>0\). In the two last cases I will shrink my original interval. If \(f(m)<0\) I will “move” \(a\) to the right: \(a=m\); if \(f(m)>0\) then \(b=m\). Note that now I have another interval \((a,b)\) whose length is half the length of the original interval, and still \(f(a)f(b)<0\). Now I can repeat the process of dividing the interval in half again. So, sooner or later I either find an \(m\) such that \(f(m)=0\), or, which is also a possibility, this process of bisecting my intervals will continue indefinitely.

In the latter case I will have two infinite sequences \[ a_0=a,\ a_1,\ a_2,\ldots,\qquad b_0=b,\ b_1,\ b_2,\ldots \] (here \(a_k,b_k\) are the values of \(a\) and \(b\) at the \(k\)-th step), such that \[ a_0\leq a_1\leq a_2\leq\ldots \] and \(b_0\geq b_1\geq b_2\geq\ldots\). Moreover, for every index \(k\) we have \(a_k\leq b\) and \(b_k\geq a\). So, both sequences \((a_k)\) and \((b_k)\) are monotone and bounded, and therefore (recall your Calculus class, or better an introductory analysis class, this is the most subtle point here) must have limits: \[ a_k\to c,\qquad b_k\to c'. \] Since \(b_k-a_k=(b-a)/2^k\), the distance between \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) goes to zero, and it must be true then that (think this out) \[ c=c'. \] Moreover, since \(f\) is continuous and \(f(a_k)<0,\,f(b_k)>0\), then \(f(a_k)\to f(c)\leq 0\) and \(f(b_k)\to f(c)\geq 0\) (note that the limit turns strict inequalities into non-strict ones). And therefore \[ f(c)=0, \] that is, we found the root \(\hat x=c\) as was claimed. ■

Why did I spend the time to present this proof? Intuitively, if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), and the signs of \(f\) are opposite at the ends of the interval, then its graph must intersect the \(x\)-axis at least once (make a drawing), no need for extended arguments. The given proof, however, is algorithmic in nature (this is not always so in mathematics, but if it happens, we should use it). In words, this proof shows that if the given conditions are true then 1) we must have a root in the given interval; and, moreover, 2) we can build a sequence of nested intervals, each of which contains this root, and the lengths of these intervals go to zero. This sounds like something that can be efficiently turned into a computer program, which I discuss next.

3.2Bisection algorithm

Never start writing your code before you have a clear understanding of the exact steps of the algorithm you want to realize, the initial data for your program, and the condition that would guarantee that your program runs in a finite time. Another aspect is to be prepared to carefully test your program even before you produced any code. Your test should be something for which you already know the exact answer. For the bisection algorithm I will take the equation \[ f(x)=x^2-2=0, \] for which \(f\) is continuous, \(f(1)=-1<0\), \(f(2)=2>0\), and therefore the interval \([1,2]\) must contain a root, and of course I know this root, which is \(\hat x=\sqrt{2}\), and which can be approximated as \[ \sqrt{2}\approx 1.41421356237309504880 \] with \(20\) correct digits after the decimal point. So in a perfect world, my program should return an answer which is close to the approximation above, and, ideally, I should also be able to somehow control the number of correct digits.

(A side remark: I hope it is clear that computers cannot in general operate with numbers like \(\sqrt{2}\), only with their rational approximations, recall the float numbers from Sections 1 and 2. I will discuss these issues later in the course; for now just do not expect to see \(\sqrt{2}\) as an output of my (or your) program. A deeper truth is that quite a few modern mathematical software packages, such as Mathematica, Maple, or SymPy in Python, are capable of performing operations with exact irrational numbers, but this is a different story.)

So, I chose my test function (usually, there should be more than one, of course, and you should always try to come up with some “difficult” examples to test your code). The initial data must be the function \(f\) itself and an interval \([a,b]\) such that \(f(a)f(b)<0\). For my test function this is the interval \([1,2]\).

The least obvious point here is to choose a condition for the program to stop. Certainly, if I am lucky enough to find an \(\hat x\) such that \(f(\hat x)=0\) I have to stop and return \(\hat x\) (which, incidentally, could be not the exact root, because \(f(\hat x)\) is also computed with rounding errors), but in most cases this situation will never happen: my intervals will be halved and halved without ever hitting exactly the point that turns \(f\) into zero.

One definite possibility is to quit the computation when the absolute value of \(f\) evaluated at some point \(\hat x\) is below some chosen threshold: \begin{equation}\label{eq3:2} |f(\hat x)|<\epsilon\implies \hat x\mbox{ is our approximation of the (unknown) root}. \end{equation} While it is one of the natural conditions for many other computations, for finding the roots of scalar equations it is not the best one. There are two reasons for this. First, even if \(|f(\hat x)|\) is very small, one can still be quite far from the root (see Fig. 3.1). Second, the knowledge of \(\epsilon\) does not tell us much about the number of correct digits our computation has produced.

Another possibility is to observe the subsequent approximations of the quantity we are after, call them \(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n,\ldots\); the stop condition can be \begin{equation}\label{eq3:3} |c_{n+1}-c_n|<\epsilon, \end{equation} which is the absolute error. Or, better for some situations, \begin{equation}\label{eq3:4} \left|\frac{c_{n+1}-c_n}{c_{n+1}}\right|<\epsilon, \end{equation} where now the relative error is computed.

All these conditions are used in various computations (as we will see later), but for the current problem there is definitely a better one. In the bisection method that I am about to implement I know for sure that my root is sitting within a given interval, whose length \(l\) is \begin{equation}\label{eq3:5} l=\frac{|b-a|}{2^n} \end{equation} at the \(n\)-th step. Hence, if I take the center of this \(n\)-th interval as my approximation of the root, the error does not exceed \(\frac{l}{2}\). Therefore, if I know my acceptable level of the error, say \(\epsilon\), I can conservatively guarantee it by taking \[ n=\left\lfloor\log_2 \frac{|b-a|}{\epsilon}\right\rfloor+1, \] where \(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\) is the floor function (it takes the integer part for positive numbers). For instance, for \([a,b]=[1,2]\) and \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\) I get \(n=\lfloor 26.58\ldots\rfloor+1=27\).

Having said all this, I now realize that I must have another piece of initial data, namely, the tolerance (the acceptable level of the error), and I will stop my computation if the length of the interval at some step is below this tolerance. To be completely on the safe side, I also would like to set a limit on the number of bisections my program performs. It could happen that, due to the way the computer represents numbers, the length of my interval will never become smaller than the given tolerance, no matter how many divisions I make; I know it sounds strange, but recall machine epsilon from Exercise 2.7: float numbers near \(\hat x\) are separated by gaps of size about \(|\hat x|\cdot 2\cdot 10^{-16}\), and no interval with float ends can be shorter than this gap. Let me take, again, very conservatively, this number to be equal to \(50\), such that my initial interval will be reduced by the factor \[ 2^{-50}\approx 10^{-15}. \] (This is an OK number if I am looking for a root not far from zero and the interval is not that large; in the case of a large interval I may need to take more iterations.)

Now everything is ready to write the code.

3.3Bisection implementation

Here is my function. First I present it, and below I will leave a few comments on the syntax I did not discuss in the previous sections.

def bisection(f, a, b, tol=1e-8, max_iterations=50):
    """Find a root of f in the interval [a, b] by bisection.

    The signs of f(a) and f(b) must be opposite. The iterations stop when
    the length of the current interval is smaller than tol, and its midpoint
    is returned; hence the error of the result does not exceed tol / 2.
    If this does not happen after max_iterations bisections, an error is raised.
    """
    if a > b:
        a, b = b, a     # make sure that a is the left end and b is the right one

    fa, fb = f(a), f(b)

    if fa == 0:
        return a

    if fb == 0:
        return b

    # check that the signs of f(a) and f(b) are opposite; I do not use the
    # product fa * fb, which may overflow or underflow
    if (fa < 0) == (fb < 0):
        raise ValueError("The signs of f at the ends of the interval should be opposite.")

    for k in range(max_iterations):
        mid = a + (b - a) / 2   # the midpoint; (a + b) / 2 may overflow for huge a, b
        fmid = f(mid)

        if fmid == 0:   # we are lucky!
            return mid

        if (fa < 0) != (fmid < 0):  # opposite signs of f at a and mid: "move" b
            b = mid                 # (fb is not used anymore, no need to update it)
        else:                       # otherwise "move" a
            a, fa = mid, fmid

        if b - a < tol:
            return a + (b - a) / 2

    raise RuntimeError(f"No convergence; the root is in [{a}, {b}].")

Here are some extended comments on the above code:

  • Note that the parameters tol and max_iterations in the definition of the function already have values: tol=1e-8 and max_iterations=50 (recall that 1e-8 is a convenient way to write \(10^{-8}=0.00000001\)). This means that these parameters have default values, and if I am satisfied with them, I do not need to provide them when calling this function. If I am not, I can write, e.g., bisection(f, 1, 2, tol=1e-12).

  • The test (fa < 0) == (fb < 0) compares two values of type bool: it is True if \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) are both negative or both positive (zeros were already dealt with). The product fa * fb would also do the job mathematically, but for very large or very small values of \(f\) the product of two float numbers can be too large (and become inf) or too small (and become exactly 0.0).

  • The variable k of the for loop is not used inside the loop; the loop is just a way to repeat the bisection at most max_iterations times. The length of the interval is checked after each bisection, so that after \(n\) bisections the length \(|b-a|/2^n\) from \(\eqref{eq3:5}\) is compared with tol; for \([1,2]\) and tol=1e-8 the function stops after exactly the 27 bisections computed above.

  • Within the code I check two possible situations when my function fails to find a root with the desired tolerance. Initially I check that the user actually submitted \(a\) and \(b\) such that \(f(a)f(b)<0\) (anyone can make a typing mistake, so I do not want my function to work on a problem that possibly has no solution). I also check whether the required tolerance is reached after all the allowed iterations. If not, I would like to make the user aware of it. In both of these situations I technically have an error: my function does not do what it is supposed to do. Therefore I decided to stop with an error message, rather than return some number. In Python I can accomplish this by using the keyword raise followed by the kind of the error. In the first case I have incorrect parameter values, and therefore I use ValueError with the message I want the user to see. In the second case the computation itself failed, so I use RuntimeError to indicate it. You can find more choices in the help system.

To compare my code with another possible realization, I copied another bisection function from a good textbook (I keep its original form; it uses the functions sign and abs from NumPy).

import numpy as np #for functions sign and abs

def bisection1(f, a, b, tol):

    if np.sign(f(a))==np.sign(f(b)):
        raise Exception("The scalars a and b do not bound a root")

    m = (a + b)/2

    if np.abs(f(m)) < tol:
        return m

    elif np.sign(f(a)) == np.sign(f(m)):
        return bisection1(f, m, b, tol)

    elif np.sign(f(b)) == np.sign(f(m)):
        return bisection1(f, a, m, tol)

This function, although it usually produces a correct answer, is somewhat suboptimal from my point of view. Here are the issues I see with the code in bisection1:

  • The function uses the so-called recursion, that is, it calls itself inside the body of the function. This is a legitimate approach, but here it makes the function work until the desired level of tolerance is reached without fixing the maximal allowable number of calls. What will happen if I set a very low tolerance? Or if the required level simply cannot be reached because of the nasty nature of the function?

  • There are multiple instances when the same function is evaluated at the same value. For instance, \(f(m)\) can be computed 3 times just within the same body; these are unnecessary computations. In general, e.g., \(f(a)\) can be computed multiple times during the execution of the code, which is pricey if the computation of \(f\) is not straightforward.

  • Using condition \(\eqref{eq3:2}\) to stop the computation does not tell us much about the number of correct digits we get.

  • There is an unnecessary comparison in the last elif. If the program gets there, the condition np.sign(f(b)) == np.sign(f(m)) will always be true.

3.4Testing

Let me test the code now. My test function is

def f1(x):
    return x * x - 2

and I try several values of the tolerance.

>>> bisection(f1, 1, 2)
1.414213564246893
>>> bisection(f1, 1, 2, tol=1e-12)
1.414213562372879
>>> bisection(f1, 1, 2, tol=1e-15)
1.4142135623730954

Comparing with \(\sqrt 2\approx1.41421356237309504880\), I see 8, 11, and 15 correct digits after the decimal point, respectively. This agrees with the guarantee that the error does not exceed tol/2: if the tolerance is \(10^{-m}\), I can expect about \(m\) correct digits after the decimal point (I say “about” because, e.g., the numbers \(1.3999999\) and \(1.4000001\) are very close, but have no common digits after the decimal point). The other implementation gives comparable results for this function, but we cannot be sure how many correct digits we get without careful analysis of the function \(f\), which has to be done on a case by case basis.

Trying a smaller tolerance, even with a larger number of iterations,

>>> bisection(f1, 1, 2, tol=1e-16, max_iterations=1000)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
RuntimeError                              Traceback (most recent call last)
...
RuntimeError: No convergence; the root is in [1.414213562373095, 1.4142135623730951].

immediately gives the error “No convergence.” This is exactly the strange situation I warned you about: the two numbers in the message are two neighboring float numbers, the distance between them is about \(2.2\cdot 10^{-16}>10^{-16}\), there are no float numbers between them, and therefore the computed midpoint always coincides with one of the ends and the interval cannot shrink anymore. If you try the same tolerance with bisection1, you will get a long error message which ends with “RecursionError: maximum recursion depth exceeded.”

Let me go back to the equation \[ \tan x= - 2x, \] and formulate the problem of finding a few first positive solutions. How do I know they exist? Because of the graphs of the functions. In general, if you are required to solve some scalar equation, it is always advisable to make a plot first (I will discuss plotting in Python later in the course; all the figures in these notes are made with Python). Here I have the picture in Fig. 3.2.

This indicates that I have infinitely many positive roots (I denote them \(\mu_j\)), such that \(\mu_1\in(\pi/2,\pi)\), \(\mu_2\in(3\pi/2,2\pi)\), etc. Note, however, that I cannot use, e.g., \(\pi/2\) as the left end of my interval, since the function is not defined at this point and the program will not run properly. I can, however, always take a close point \(\pi/2+\delta\) for some small positive \(\delta\).

import math


def f2(x):
    return math.tan(x) + 2 * x
>>> bisection(f2, math.pi / 2 + 0.01, math.pi)
1.8365972056496869

The correct answer for \(\mu_1\) (found with the Python package mpmath, which can compute with any prescribed number of digits, here 30) is \(1.8365972031521257227\), which again shows that I can be sure about 8 digits after the decimal point.

Now let me try to work with a discontinuous function: \[ f(x)=\begin{cases} 1,&x\geq 0,\\ -1,&x<0. \end{cases} \]

def sign(x):
    if x < 0:
        return -1
    else:
        return 1
>>> bisection(sign, -1, 1)
-3.725290298461914e-09
>>> bisection1(sign, -1, 1, 1e-3)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
RecursionError                            Traceback (most recent call last)
...
RecursionError: maximum recursion depth exceeded

This shows yet another advantage of the chosen stop condition for the computations. My function actually identified correctly the discontinuity point of the function (which sometimes can be as important as finding actual roots). The other routine again reached the maximum level of recursion and stopped, since \(|f(m)|=1\) is never smaller than tol.

Clearly, it is also possible to have functions which are discontinuous and unbounded, such as \(f(x)=1/x\).

def reciprocal(x):
    return 1 / x
>>> bisection(reciprocal, -1, 2)
-9.313225746154785e-10
>>> reciprocal(-9.313225746154785e-10)
-1073741824.0

Again, my function returns an answer very close to zero. Plugging this answer back into the function, however, I realize that I am dealing with an unlimited growth and not with a root. (On the interval \([-1,1]\) the very first midpoint is exactly 0, and Python stops with ZeroDivisionError: division by zero, which is also a hint that something is wrong.) In a nutshell, one should always tinker with the problem to be sure that the found result makes sense.

3.5Conclusions

In this section I discussed the general problem of root finding and one specific method: bisection. The clear advantage of this method is that, if properly implemented and applied to a right object, it is doomed to succeed. Why would we need something else? The truth to be told: this method is ridiculously slow, especially if one needs to solve thousands of problems with a high degree of accuracy. In the next section I will introduce a few other methods, which you will analyze using Python. As a piece of advice, however, I would like to finish this section with the following sentiment: if in your actual activity you need to solve just one specific equation \(f(x)=0\) and you surely know that the root you are looking for is within certain \(a\) and \(b\), then use bisection, its elementary character and slow convergence notwithstanding. It will always lead you in the right direction, slowly yet unavoidably.
